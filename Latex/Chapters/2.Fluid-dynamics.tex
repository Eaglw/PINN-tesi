\chapter{Viscoelastic Fluid Mechanics and the Four-Roll Mill}
\label{chap:fluidodynamic_background}

A polymer solution flowing through a four-roll mill behaves very differently from a Newtonian solvent.
Macromolecular chains carry memory of their past deformation history,
generating elastic stresses that persist across recirculation paths.
In this chapter, we develop the continuum mechanics framework required to model these non-linear flows.

We begin from the universal conservation principles of mass and linear momentum,
introducing the kinematic simplifications appropriate for low-inertia polymer solutions.
We then formulate the constitutive equations governing viscoelastic fluids,
starting from the linear Oldroyd-B model and the Upper-Convected Time Derivative (UCTD),
and highlighting the kinematic origins of the High-Weissenberg Number Problem (HWNP).
Next, we present the non-linear Giesekus and Phan-Thien--Tanner (PTT) formulations,
which introduce stress-limiting mechanisms that bound extensional stress growth.
Finally, we nondimensionalize the system using a viscous stress scale,
characterize the four-roll mill geometry and its extensional stagnation kinematics,
and define the forward and inverse problem formulations analyzed in this thesis.

% ============================================================
% 2.1 GENERAL CONSERVATION LAWS
% ============================================================
\section{General Conservation Laws}
\label{sec:general_conservation_laws}

From a continuum perspective, fluid motion is governed by the universal conservation laws
of mass and linear momentum \cite{bird1987dynamics}.

\subsection{Principle of Mass Conservation}
\label{subsec:general_mass}
For a fluid with local density $\rho(\boldsymbol{x}, t)$ and velocity vector $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}, t)$,
conservation of mass is expressed by the continuity equation:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \boldsymbol{u}) = 0 .
    \label{eq:general_mass}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:general_mass} states that the local rate of mass accumulation
within a control volume balances the net convective mass flux through its boundaries.

\subsection{Conservation of Linear Momentum}
\label{subsec:general_momentum}
Applying Newton's second law to an infinitesimal fluid parcel yields the momentum balance
in Eulerian coordinates:
\begin{equation}
    \rho \left( \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{u}}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla \boldsymbol{u} \right) = \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} + \rho \boldsymbol{g} ,
    \label{eq:general_momentum}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{g}$ represents external body force accelerations,
and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ is the symmetric second-order total Cauchy stress tensor.
The left-hand side is the material derivative $\mathrm{D}\boldsymbol{u}/\mathrm{D}t$,
combining local unsteadiness $\partial \boldsymbol{u}/\partial t$ with convective acceleration $\boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla \boldsymbol{u}$.

To isolate the isotropic pressure from deviatoric deformation stresses,
we partition the total stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ as:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\sigma} = -p \boldsymbol{I} + \boldsymbol{T} ,
    \label{eq:cauchy_stress_decomp}
\end{equation}
where $p$ is the isotropic hydrodynamic pressure,
$\boldsymbol{I}$ is the second-order identity tensor,
and $\boldsymbol{T}$ is the extra-stress tensor containing all viscous and elastic contributions.
Substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:cauchy_stress_decomp} into Equation~\eqref{eq:general_momentum} yields:
\begin{equation}
    \rho \left( \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{u}}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla \boldsymbol{u} \right) = -\nabla p + \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{T} + \rho \boldsymbol{g} .
    \label{eq:general_momentum_expanded}
\end{equation}

% 2.2 PHYSICAL ASSUMPTIONS AND GOVERNING SYSTEM REDUCTION
% ============================================================
\section{Physical Assumptions and Governing System Reduction}
\label{sec:physical_assumptions}

The full three-dimensional, unsteady equations of motion can be reduced by
introducing scale-appropriate physical assumptions for polymer solutions within the
four-roll mill geometry:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Continuous medium and isotropic rest state}:
    The fluid is modeled as a continuous medium that remains locally isotropic at rest.
    Flow deformation stretches and aligns macromolecular chains,
    producing stress anisotropies represented by the extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{T}$.

    \item \textbf{Incompressibility and stream function formulation}:
    Under the moderate operating pressures and low flow velocities considered here,
    density variations are negligible ($\rho = \text{const}$).
    The continuity equation~\eqref{eq:general_mass} simplifies to:
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 0 .
        \label{eq:incompressibility}
    \end{equation}
    In two-dimensional planar motion, we satisfy incompressibility identically
    by introducing a scalar stream function $\psi(x, y)$ such that:
    \begin{equation}
        u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}, \qquad v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} .
        \label{eq:stream_function_def}
    \end{equation}
    Because mixed partial derivatives commute ($\partial^2 \psi / \partial x \partial y = \partial^2 \psi / \partial y \partial x$),
    the velocity field satisfies $\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u} \equiv 0$ everywhere.

    \item \textbf{Isothermal operating conditions}:
    Internal temperature rises due to viscous heating remain negligible under our operating conditions.
    The energy equation decouples from the flow kinematics,
    and fluid transport properties (viscosities and relaxation times) are treated as constants.

    \item \textbf{Absence of body forces}:
    The four-roll mill operates in a horizontal cross-sectional plane ($x, y$).
    Gravitational acceleration acts along the vertical cylinder axis ($z$),
    perpendicular to the flow plane, so we set $\boldsymbol{g} = \boldsymbol{0}$.

    \item \textbf{Steady-state regime}:
    We focus on the established steady operating regime,
    setting all local temporal derivatives to zero ($\partial(\cdot)/\partial t = \boldsymbol{0}$).

    \item \textbf{Two-dimensional planar kinematics}:
    Given the high aspect ratio of the cylindrical rollers along the $z$-axis,
    axial end effects near the transverse mid-plane are negligible.
    The velocity field is modeled as strictly planar:
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{u} = (u(x, y), \, v(x, y), \, 0) .
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

Under these physical simplifications, the steady momentum balance reduces to:
\begin{equation}
    \rho (\boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla \boldsymbol{u}) = -\nabla p + \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{T} .
    \label{eq:momentum_reduced_general}
\end{equation}

% 2.3 CONSTITUTIVE MODELING OF VISCOELASTIC FLUIDS
% ============================================================
\section{Constitutive Modeling of Viscoelastic Fluids}
\label{sec:constitutive_models}

In two dimensions, the momentum balance~\eqref{eq:momentum_reduced_general} contains
six unknown scalar fields ($u, v, p, T_{xx}, T_{xy}, T_{yy}$),
while mass and momentum conservation provide only three equations.
A constitutive equation for the extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{T}$ is required to close the system.

For dilute and semi-dilute polymer solutions,
we partition the extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{T}$ into a Newtonian solvent contribution
and an elastic polymeric contribution $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ \cite{bird1987dynamics, macosko1994rheology}:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{T} = 2\mu_s \boldsymbol{D} + \boldsymbol{\tau} ,
    \label{eq:stress_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_s$ is the dynamic viscosity of the Newtonian solvent,
and $\boldsymbol{D}$ is the symmetric rate-of-strain tensor:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{D} = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \nabla \boldsymbol{u} + (\nabla \boldsymbol{u})^T \right] .
    \label{eq:strain_rate_tensor}
\end{equation}
This decomposition isolates the instantaneous viscous dissipation of the solvent ($2\mu_s \boldsymbol{D}$)
from the delayed elastic stress $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ generated by macromolecular stretching and orientation.
Substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:stress_decomposition} into Equation~\eqref{eq:momentum_reduced_general}
and exploiting incompressibility ($\nabla \cdot (2\mu_s \boldsymbol{D}) = \mu_s \nabla^2 \boldsymbol{u}$),
the steady momentum equation becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \rho (\boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla \boldsymbol{u}) = -\nabla p + \mu_s \nabla^2 \boldsymbol{u} + \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau} .
    \label{eq:momentum_reduced}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Upper-Convected Time Derivative}
\label{subsec:uctd_convention}
Polymeric fluids exhibit fading memory:
macromolecules orient and stretch under flow and relax over finite timescales.
As a fluid parcel moves through the domain,
its internal stress is advected along streamlines, rotated by local vorticity,
and stretched by velocity gradients.

To satisfy material frame-indifference,
constitutive equations must employ an objective tensor rate.
For polymer chains deforming affinely with the continuum,
the appropriate objective rate is the \textit{Upper-Convected Time Derivative} (UCTD),
denoted by $\overset{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}$.
With the velocity gradient defined as $(\nabla \boldsymbol{u})_{ij} = \partial u_i / \partial x_j$,
the UCTD of the symmetric extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \overset{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{\tau}} \equiv \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\tau}}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla \boldsymbol{\tau} - (\nabla \boldsymbol{u}) \boldsymbol{\tau} - \boldsymbol{\tau} (\nabla \boldsymbol{u})^T .
    \label{eq:uctd_definition}
\end{equation}
Under steady conditions, $\partial \boldsymbol{\tau}/\partial t = \boldsymbol{0}$.
The term $\boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla \boldsymbol{\tau}$ describes convective stress transport,
while $-(\nabla \boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{\tau} - \boldsymbol{\tau}(\nabla \boldsymbol{u})^T$
captures stretching and rotation induced by local velocity gradients.

\subsection{The Oldroyd-B Model and Extensional Behavior}
\label{subsec:oldroyd_b}
The classical Oldroyd-B model \cite{oldroyd1950formulation} idealizes macromolecules
as non-interacting Hookean dumbbells suspended in a Newtonian solvent \cite{bird1987dynamics}.
Kinetic theory relates the polymeric viscosity $\mu_p$ directly to the relaxation time $\lambda$:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_p = n k_B T \lambda ,
    \label{eq:kinetic_viscosity_relation}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is polymer number density, $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant, and $T$ is absolute temperature.
Under steady conditions, the polymeric extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\tau} + \lambda \overset{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{\tau}} = 2\mu_p \boldsymbol{D} .
    \label{eq:oldroyd_b_dimensional}
\end{equation}
The total zero-shear viscosity of the solution is $\mu_0 = \mu_s + \mu_p$.

While Oldroyd-B predicts a constant shear viscosity $\eta(\dot{\gamma}) = \mu_0$ (characteristic of Boger fluids),
it exhibits a well-known singularity in steady planar extensional flow ($u = E x, v = -E y$, with $E > 0$).
As the strain rate approaches the critical threshold:
\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{crit}} = \frac{1}{2\lambda} ,
    \label{eq:ecrit}
\end{equation}
the assumption of infinitely extendable Hookean springs allows unbounded stretching.
Consequently, extensional stresses and extensional viscosity diverge to infinity ($\eta_E \to \infty$).
At low deformation rates ($E \to 0$), the planar extensional viscosity recovers the Newtonian limit,
giving a planar Trouton ratio $\mathrm{Tr}_E = \eta_E / \mu_0 \to 4$.

This stress singularity near $2\lambda E \to 1$ is the constitutive root of the
\textit{High-Weissenberg Number Problem} (HWNP),
which creates steep stress boundary layers that challenge conventional grid-based solvers \cite{owens2002computational}.

\subsection{Non-linear Closures: Giesekus and Phan-Thien--Tanner (PTT)}
\label{subsec:nonlinear_models}
To avoid unbounded stress divergence and capture the behavior of concentrated solutions,
non-linear constitutive models introduce stress-limiting mechanisms:

\subsubsection{The Giesekus Model}
The Giesekus model \cite{giesekus1982simple, bird1987dynamics} accounts for anisotropic hydrodynamic drag
between neighboring macromolecules by adding a quadratic stress relaxation term:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\tau} + \lambda \overset{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{\tau}} + \frac{\alpha \lambda}{\mu_p} \boldsymbol{\tau}^2 = 2\mu_p \boldsymbol{D} ,
    \label{eq:giesekus_dimensional}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \in [0, 0.5]$ is the dimensionless mobility parameter,
and the matrix square is $(\boldsymbol{\tau}^2)_{ij} = \tau_{ik}\tau_{kj}$.
When $\alpha = 0$, the model recovers Oldroyd-B.
For $\alpha > 0$, the quadratic term bounds extensional stress growth and introduces shear-thinning in steady shear.

\subsubsection{The Phan-Thien--Tanner (PTT) Model}
Derived from transient network theory \cite{phanthien1977new, bird1987dynamics},
the linear PTT model assumes that entanglement creation and destruction rates depend on stored elastic stress:
\begin{equation}
    f(\boldsymbol{\tau}) \boldsymbol{\tau} + \lambda \overset{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{\tau}} = 2\mu_p \boldsymbol{D} ,
    \label{eq:ptt_dimensional}
\end{equation}
with the stress-softening function:
\begin{equation}
    f(\boldsymbol{\tau}) = 1 + \frac{\varepsilon \lambda}{\mu_p} \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau}) ,
    \label{eq:linear_ptt}
\end{equation}
where $\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau}) = \tau_{xx} + \tau_{yy}$,
and $\varepsilon \ge 0$ is the extensibility parameter governing stress-induced entanglement breakdown.
When $\varepsilon = 0$, $f = 1$ and the model recovers Oldroyd-B.
For $\varepsilon > 0$, the relaxation rate increases with stress, bounding extensional viscosity.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{media/rheological_models_concept.png}
    \caption{Conceptual comparison of constitutive model responses: (a) steady shear viscosity $\eta(\dot{\gamma})$, showing the constant viscosity of Oldroyd-B versus the shear-thinning behavior of Giesekus ($\alpha > 0$); (b) planar extensional viscosity $\eta_E(E)$, contrasting the asymptotic stress divergence of Oldroyd-B at $E_{\mathrm{crit}} = 1/(2\lambda)$ with the bounded extensional plateau of the Giesekus formulation.}
    \label{fig:rheological_models_concept}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Focus of Present Investigation: Oldroyd-B and Giesekus}
In this dissertation, we focus our computational investigations on two representative models:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Oldroyd-B}: The standard linear benchmark for constant-viscosity Boger fluids,
    capturing purely elastic normal stress growth and the High-Weissenberg Number Problem.
    \item \textbf{Giesekus}: The primary non-linear model,
    incorporating mobility $\alpha \in (0, 0.5]$ to capture shear-thinning and bounded extensional stress.
\end{enumerate}
We keep the PTT formulation in our governing differential residuals for generality,
setting it to $\varepsilon = 0$ during baseline benchmarks
and using it in Section~\ref{subsec:ptt_degeneracy_analysis} to study parameter identifiability limits.

\section{Nondimensionalization and Governing Equations}
\label{sec:nondimensionalization}

Nondimensionalization scales physical variables to order unity and establishes the
governing dimensionless groups \cite{owens2002computational}. In low-Reynolds
viscoelastic flows, scaling by a viscous stress unit rather than an inertial dynamic
pressure improves numerical conditioning and balances the magnitudes of the governing
differential operators.

\subsection{Reference Scales and Dimensionless Groups}
We select reference length $H_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (roller radius $R$), reference velocity
$U_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (roller surface speed $u_{\mathrm{roll}}$), and a constant
reference viscosity $\eta_0$ (chosen as a fixed baseline scale, e.g., $\eta_0 =
1.0\,\mathrm{Pa\cdot s}$). The dimensionless variables are defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{x}^{*} = \frac{\boldsymbol{x}}{H_{\mathrm{ref}}}, \quad
    \boldsymbol{u}^{*} = \frac{\boldsymbol{u}}{U_{\mathrm{ref}}}, \quad
    p^{*} = \frac{p}{\frac{\eta_0 U_{\mathrm{ref}}}{H_{\mathrm{ref}}}}, \quad
    \boldsymbol{\tau}^{*} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\tau}}{\frac{\eta_0 U_{\mathrm{ref}}}{H_{\mathrm{ref}}}} .
    \label{eq:dimless_variables_viscous}
\end{equation}
Dropping asterisks for notational brevity, the governing non-dimensional groups are:
\begin{align}
    Re_{\mathrm{scale}} &= \frac{\rho U_{\mathrm{ref}} H_{\mathrm{ref}}}{\eta_0} \quad &\text{(Scaling Reynolds number)}, \label{eq:reynolds_scale} \\
    Re_{\mathrm{phys}} &= \frac{\rho U_{\mathrm{ref}} H_{\mathrm{ref}}}{\mu_s + \mu_p} = \frac{Re_{\mathrm{scale}}}{\mu_s^* + \mu_p^*} \quad &\text{(Physical Reynolds number)}, \label{eq:reynolds_phys} \\
    Wi &= \frac{\lambda U_{\mathrm{ref}}}{H_{\mathrm{ref}}} \quad &\text{(Global Weissenberg number)}, \label{eq:weissenberg} \\
    \beta &= \frac{\mu_s}{\mu_s + \mu_p} = \frac{\mu_s^*}{\mu_s^* + \mu_p^*} \quad &\text{(Viscosity ratio)}, \label{eq:viscosity_ratio} \\
    \mu_s^* &= \frac{\mu_s}{\eta_0}, \qquad \mu_p^* = \frac{\mu_p}{\eta_0} \quad &\text{(Dimensionless solvent and polymer viscosities)} . \label{eq:viscosities_star}
\end{align}
The distinction between $Re_{\mathrm{scale}}$ and $Re_{\mathrm{phys}}$ is essential in inverse problems.
Defining the momentum equation through the constant scale $Re_{\mathrm{scale}}$ ensures that
the numerical scaling of convective acceleration remains invariant
even as unknown fluid viscosities ($\mu_s, \mu_p$) are adjusted during optimization.

\subsection{Dimensionless Vector Formulation}
The non-dimensional governing equations in steady 2D flow become:
\begin{align}
    \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u} &= 0 , \label{eq:dimless_mass} \\
    Re_{\mathrm{scale}} (\boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla \boldsymbol{u}) + \nabla p - \mu_s^* \nabla^2 \boldsymbol{u} - \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau} &= \boldsymbol{0} , \label{eq:dimless_momentum} \\
    \boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}) + Wi \left( \boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla \boldsymbol{\tau} - (\nabla \boldsymbol{u}) \boldsymbol{\tau} - \boldsymbol{\tau} (\nabla \boldsymbol{u})^T \right) - 2\mu_p^* \boldsymbol{D} &= \boldsymbol{0} , \label{eq:dimless_constitutive_unified}
\end{align}
where the constitutive operator $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})$ unifies
the models under study:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}) = 
    \begin{cases}
        \boldsymbol{\tau} & \text{(Oldroyd-B)}, \\[1ex]
        \boldsymbol{\tau} + \frac{\alpha Wi}{\mu_p^*} \boldsymbol{\tau}^2 & \text{(Giesekus)}, \\[1ex]
        \left(1 + \frac{\varepsilon Wi}{\mu_p^*} \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\right) \boldsymbol{\tau} & \text{(Linear PTT)} .
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:constitutive_operator_cases}
\end{equation}

\section{The Four-Roll Mill Benchmark Kinematics and Boundaries}
\label{sec:four_roll_mill_bcs}

The four-roll mill is a canonical benchmark designed to generate controlled two-dimensional planar extensional flow
with a central stagnation point \cite{taylor1934formation, fuller1980flow, fuller1981flow, bentley1986experimental}.
Originally conceived by Taylor \cite{taylor1934formation} and systematically developed by Fuller and Leal \cite{fuller1980flow, fuller1981flow},
it has served as a standard apparatus for analyzing macromolecular deformation and flow-induced birefringence.
As depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:4roll_geometry},
the setup consists of four cylindrical rollers placed symmetrically inside a square domain.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.68\linewidth]{media/4roll_mill_geometry_thesis.png}
    \caption{Schematic representation of the four-roll mill geometry, illustrating
    the square cavity of side $L$, cylindrical rollers of radius $R$ at offset
    $d_{\mathrm{roll}}$, Cartesian axes $(x,y)$, rotation directions with angular
    velocity $\Omega$ (adjacent rolls counter-rotate, diagonal rolls co-rotate), and
    the central hyperbolic stagnation point $(0,0)$.}
    \label{fig:4roll_geometry}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Kinematic Relations and Flow Classification}
\label{subsec:4roll_kinematics}
In the vicinity of the central region between the rollers,
we represent the local velocity field through a linear superposition of extension and rotation
\cite{fuller1980flow, fuller1981flow}:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{u}(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} E x + \omega y \\ -E y - \omega x \end{pmatrix} ,
    \label{eq:kinematic_general}
\end{equation}
where $E \ge 0$ denotes the extension rate and $\omega \ge 0$ represents the local angular rotation rate.
The associated scalar vorticity is:
\begin{equation}
    \omega_z = (\nabla \times \boldsymbol{u})_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -2\omega .
\end{equation}
To quantify the relative magnitude of extension versus rotation,
we define a dimensionless flow type parameter $\chi \in [-1, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
    \chi = \frac{E - \omega}{E + \omega} .
    \label{eq:flow_parameter_chi}
\end{equation}
Depending on roller speeds and rotation directions,
this idealized local kinematics captures three canonical flow regimes
(illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:four_roll_flow_regimes}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\chi = 1$ ($\omega = 0$): \textbf{Pure Planar Extensional Flow}.
    Generated when adjacent rolls counter-rotate symmetrically (diagonal rolls co-rotate).
    Fluid elements experience pure stretching along $x$ and pure compression along $y$,
    with hyperbolic streamlines $x y = \text{const}$.
    \item $\chi = 0$ ($E = \omega$): \textbf{Simple Shear Flow}.
    An intermediate state where rate of strain and angular rotation have equal magnitude ($E = \omega$),
    producing rectilinear shear streamlines.
    In an enclosed cavity, this uniform shear field is realized locally near the center,
    while further away the flow curves to adapt to roller and wall boundaries.
    \item $\chi = -1$ ($E = 0$): \textbf{Pure Solid-Body Rotation}.
    Generated when rolls co-rotate to induce bulk circulation.
    This yields concentric circular streamlines around the origin,
    representing rigid-body rotation without elongation or compression.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Central Hyperbolic Stagnation Point}
\label{subsec:stagnation_point}
In the extensional regime ($\chi = 1$), the velocity vanishes at the geometric center:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{u}(0, 0) = \boldsymbol{0} .
\end{equation}
The velocity gradient tensor at the origin,
\begin{equation}
    \nabla \boldsymbol{u} = \begin{pmatrix} E & 0 \\ 0 & -E \end{pmatrix} ,
\end{equation}
possesses real eigenvalues $+E$ (along the horizontal stretching axis $x$)
and $-E$ (along the vertical compression axis $y$),
defining a hyperbolic saddle-point geometry.
Along the incoming vertical streamline ($x = 0$),
fluid parcels decelerate toward zero velocity,
providing fluid elements with long residence times.

This kinematic feature allows macromolecular deformation to accumulate over timescales
comparable to or larger than the fluid relaxation time $\lambda$.
Consequently, this setup provides an ideal testbed for observing elastic stress growth away from solid boundaries.

\subsection{Geometric Setup and Operating Parameters}
\label{subsec:geometry_params}
Following the established benchmark setup of D'Avino et al. \cite{davino2017numerical},
we define the physical configuration through:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cavity dimensions}: Square enclosure of side $L = 50\,\mathrm{mm}$ spanning $[-25, 25]\,\mathrm{mm}$.
    \item \textbf{Cylindrical rollers}: Radius $R = 5\,\mathrm{mm}$ ($H_{\mathrm{ref}} = R = 0.1 L$).
    \item \textbf{Roller placement}: Centers positioned symmetrically at $(\pm 10, \pm 10)\,\mathrm{mm}$ ($d_{\mathrm{roll}} = 10\,\mathrm{mm}$).
    \item \textbf{Roller kinematics}: Peripheral surface velocity $u_{\mathrm{roll}} = \Omega R = 8.333 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
    \item \textbf{Non-dimensional domain}: In units of $H_{\mathrm{ref}} = R$,
    the computational domain spans $[-5, 5]^2$,
    with roller centers located at $(\pm 2.0, \pm 2.0)$ and non-dimensional roller radius $R^* = 1.0$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Diagnostic Verification Cutlines}
\label{subsec:diagnostic_cutlines}
To enable rigorous, high-resolution validation of kinematic and stress profiles across the domain, 
we establish three representative diagnostic cutlines (Figure~\ref{fig:four_roll_cutlines_schema}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Horizontal Cutline} ($y = 0, \, -L/2 \le x \le L/2$):
    Traversing the central extensional outflow axis through the hyperbolic stagnation point $(0, 0)$.
    Along this path, fluid accelerates outward, forming intense elongational stress tails.
    \item \textbf{Vertical Cutline} ($x = 0, \, -L/2 \le y \le L/2$):
    Traversing the compressive inflow axis,
    capturing fluid deceleration into the central stagnation zone.
    \item \textbf{Oblique Diagonal Cutline} ($y = x$):
    Connecting opposite cavity corners ($[-L/2, -L/2]$ to $[L/2, L/2]$) and traversing the diagonal sectors.
    Because this trajectory intersects the solid cores of the top-right and bottom-left rollers
    (centered at $(\pm 10, \pm 10)\,\mathrm{mm}$),
    spatial evaluations are sampled strictly within the active fluid domain.
    This captures the transition from the central extensional core into the high-gradient shear layers adjacent to roller surfaces.
\end{enumerate}
These diagnostic trajectories serve as spatial evaluation baselines for both the reference CFD mesh convergence 
study and the PINN field validation detailed in Chapter~\ref{chap:results_discussion}.

\subsection{Boundary Conditions and Pressure Point Anchoring}
\label{subsec:boundary_conditions}
To complete the continuous boundary value formulation,
we specify physical boundary conditions along all solid surfaces:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tangential Velocity on Roller Walls ($\Gamma_{\mathrm{rolls}}$)}:
    On the surface of each roller $k \in \{\mathrm{TL}, \mathrm{TR}, \mathrm{BL}, \mathrm{BR}\}$,
    the fluid velocity satisfies no-slip with prescribed peripheral rotation:
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{u}|_{\Gamma_k} = \boldsymbol{\Omega}_k \times (\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{x}_k) ,
        \label{eq:bc_velocity_rolls}
    \end{equation}
    where $\boldsymbol{x}_k$ is the roller center.
    For the planar extensional regime ($\chi = 1$),
    adjacent rolls counter-rotate while diagonal rolls co-rotate:
    top-left and bottom-right rolls rotate clockwise ($-\Omega$),
    while top-right and bottom-left rolls rotate counter-clockwise ($+\Omega$).

    \item \textbf{No-Slip Velocity on Outer Cavity Walls ($\Gamma_{\mathrm{outer}}$)}:
    Along the stationary external boundaries of the square enclosure, the velocity vanishes:
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{u}|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{outer}}} = \boldsymbol{0} .
        \label{eq:bc_outer_walls}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Pressure Point Anchoring}:
    Because pressure appears in the momentum balance~\eqref{eq:dimless_momentum} exclusively through its gradient $\nabla p$,
    in an enclosed cavity with prescribed velocity Dirichlet boundary conditions
    pressure is determined only up to an arbitrary additive constant ($p \to p + C$).
    To fix this gauge freedom and obtain a unique solution,
    we specify a single reference pressure point:
    \begin{equation}
        p(\boldsymbol{x}_0) = 0 ,
        \label{eq:pressure_point}
    \end{equation}
    at an arbitrary reference coordinate $\boldsymbol{x}_0$ within the domain.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem Formulation and Computational Transition}
\label{sec:summary_mathematical_roles}

The physical laws, constitutive closures, and boundary constraints established in this chapter
define two complementary problem settings:

\subsection{Forward and Inverse Problem Formulations}
\label{subsec:forward_inverse_definitions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Forward (Direct) Problem}:
    Given a complete set of known fluid parameters ($\lambda, \mu_p, \mu_s, \alpha, \varepsilon$)
    and prescribed kinematic boundary conditions on rolls and walls,
    determine the velocity field $\boldsymbol{u}(x, y)$, pressure field $p(x, y)$,
    and polymeric extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}(x, y)$ that satisfy momentum balance~\eqref{eq:dimless_momentum}
    and constitutive closure~\eqref{eq:dimless_constitutive_unified} throughout cavity domain $\Omega$.
    \item \textbf{Inverse (Parameter Identification) Problem}:
    Given partial kinematic observations of the velocity field within the cavity,
    reconstruct the latent internal fields $(p, \boldsymbol{\tau})$
    and identify the unknown physical parameters governing the fluid
    ($\lambda, \mu_p, \mu_s$, alongside non-linear parameters $\alpha$ or $\varepsilon$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Transition to Physics-Informed Neural Networks}
\label{subsec:transition_chapter3}
The differential system formulated in Equations~\eqref{eq:dimless_mass}--\eqref{eq:constitutive_operator_cases}
represents a highly coupled, non-linear system of partial differential equations.
In Chapter~\ref{chap:pinn_literature},
we translate this continuous physical formulation into a machine-learning framework using Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs).
By embedding momentum and constitutive equations directly into the neural network loss function through automatic differentiation,
the PINN framework enables both forward flow reconstruction and inverse rheological parameter discovery
without requiring traditional spatial discretization grids.
