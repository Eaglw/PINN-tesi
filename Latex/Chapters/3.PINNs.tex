\chapter{Physics-Informed Neural Networks in Complex Rheology}
\label{chap:pinn_literature}

In this chapter, we present the theoretical and computational foundations of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and their application to complex, viscoelastic fluid mechanics. The discussion is structured logically into five main sections. First, we outline the fundamental principles of deep learning, covering artificial neurons, multi-layer perceptrons, activation functions, and gradient-based optimization algorithms. Second, we examine why neural networks represent a compelling paradigm for scientific computing, while critically assessing the structural limitations of purely data-driven black-box architectures. Third, we formalize the PINN framework, providing an in-depth comparative analysis between conventional supervised training and physics-informed optimization, detailing how Automatic Differentiation (AD) enables an exact, mesh-free formulation that naturally suppresses overfitting. Fourth, we review seminal benchmarks from the literature, focusing on the foundational Navier--Stokes cylinder flow by Raissi et al. \cite{raissi2019physics} and the ViscoelasticNet framework by Thakur et al. \cite{thakur2024viscoelasticnet}. Finally, we identify the literature gap and delineate the specific methodological contributions of this dissertation.

\section{Fundamentals of Deep Learning}
\label{sec:dl_fundamentals}

Deep learning is a subfield of machine learning based on artificial neural networks that learn hierarchical representations of continuous mathematical functions through composable layers of non-linear transformations \cite{goodfellow2016deep}.

\subsection{The Artificial Neuron and Non-Linear Activation Functions}
\label{subsec:perceptron_activations}
The elementary computational unit of a neural network is the artificial neuron (perceptron). Given an input vector $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}$, the neuron computes an affine linear transformation followed by an element-wise non-linear scalar mapping:
\begin{equation}
    z = \boldsymbol{w}^T \boldsymbol{x} + b , \qquad a = \sigma(z) ,
    \label{eq:single_neuron}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ denotes the synaptic weight vector, $b \in \mathbb{R}$ represents the scalar bias, and $\sigma(\cdot): \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is the activation function. 

The activation function $\sigma$ imparts non-linearity to the network, enabling it to approximate complex, non-linear physical operators. While piece-wise linear activations such as the Rectified Linear Unit ($\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$) dominate computer vision, they are unsuitable for differential equations because their second-order spatial derivative vanishes identically everywhere ($\sigma''(z) = 0$ for $z \ne 0$). In physics-informed modeling, activation functions must possess smooth, continuous higher-order derivatives ($\sigma \in \mathcal{C}^k$ with $k \ge 2$). In this work, we employ:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hyperbolic Tangent ($\tanh$)}:
    \begin{equation}
        \tanh(z) = \frac{e^z - e^{-z}}{e^z + e^{-z}} ,
        \label{eq:tanh_activation}
    \end{equation}
    smooth and symmetric around zero, widely used in early PINN formulations \cite{raissi2019physics}.
    \item \textbf{Sigmoid Linear Unit (SiLU / Swish)}:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{SiLU}(z) = z \cdot \text{sigmoid}(z) = \frac{z}{1 + e^{-z}} .
        \label{eq:silu_activation}
    \end{equation}
    The SiLU activation function \cite{goodfellow2016deep} is strictly smooth, non-monotonic, self-gated, and possesses continuous second and third derivatives. It exhibits superior gradient propagation properties in deep networks, preventing gradient saturation in high-curvature viscoelastic shear layers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Multi-Layer Perceptron Architecture}
\label{subsec:mlp_architecture}
By arranging neurons into sequential layers, a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) is constructed. An $L$-layer deep neural network maps an input coordinate vector $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ to an output field prediction $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}$ through the composition:
\begin{align}
    \boldsymbol{h}^{(0)} &= \boldsymbol{x} , \nonumber \\
    \boldsymbol{h}^{(l)} &= \sigma \left( \boldsymbol{W}^{(l)} \boldsymbol{h}^{(l-1)} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(l)} \right) , \quad \text{for } l = 1, \dots, L-1 , \label{eq:mlp_layers} \\
    \hat{\boldsymbol{u}} &= \boldsymbol{W}^{(L)} \boldsymbol{h}^{(L-1)} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(L)} , \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol{W}^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_l \times N_{l-1}}$ and $\boldsymbol{b}^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_l}$ denote the weight matrix and bias vector of the $l$-th hidden layer, with $N_l$ representing the layer width. The total collection of trainable parameters is denoted by $\boldsymbol{\theta} = \{ \boldsymbol{W}^{(l)}, \boldsymbol{b}^{(l)} \}_{l=1}^L$. The final layer is typically linear to ensure an unbounded range for continuous physical quantities.

To avoid vanishing or exploding gradients during backpropagation, network weights are initialized using the Xavier/Glorot Normal distribution \cite{goodfellow2016deep}:
\begin{equation}
    W_{ij}^{(l)} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \, \frac{2}{N_{l-1} + N_l}\right) .
\end{equation}
For physical outputs representing stress and pressure, zero-initialization of the final layer weights ($\boldsymbol{W}^{(L)} = \boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{b}^{(L)} = \boldsymbol{0}$) is strategically adopted to anchor initial predictions to zero prior to optimization.

\subsection{Loss Functions and Optimization Algorithms}
\label{subsec:optimization_solvers}
Training an MLP consists of solving an unconstrained non-convex optimization problem over the parameter space:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta}^* = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) ,
    \label{eq:opt_problem}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ measures the discrepancy between predicted and target quantities, typically evaluated via the Mean Squared Error (MSE):
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) - \boldsymbol{u}_i^{\mathrm{target}} \right\|_2^2 .
    \label{eq:mse_loss}
\end{equation}

Optimization is performed using gradient-based algorithms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{First-Order Stochastic Gradient Descent (Adam)}: The Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) optimizer \cite{kingma2014adam} dynamically adjusts individual learning rates for each parameter by computing exponentially decaying moving averages of past gradients ($m_t$) and squared gradients ($v_t$):
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \epsilon} \hat{m}_t ,
    \end{equation}
    where $\eta$ is the learning rate. To balance rapid exploration with asymptotic refinement, $\eta$ is scheduled using a Cosine Annealing decay:
    \begin{equation}
        \eta_t = \eta_{\min} + \frac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min}) \left( 1 + \cos\left( \frac{\pi t}{T_{\max}} \right) \right) .
        \label{eq:cosine_annealing}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Second-Order Quasi-Newton Optimization (L-BFGS)}: While Adam navigates complex, non-convex loss landscapes efficiently, it exhibits slow convergence near shallow minima. The Limited-memory Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno (L-BFGS) algorithm approximates the inverse Hessian matrix using past gradient evaluations, achieving super-linear convergence. In physics-informed settings, L-BFGS paired with a Strong Wolfe line search is essential for driving PDE residuals to scientific precision ($10^{-5} - 10^{-7}$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Backpropagation and Computational Graphs}
\label{subsec:backpropagation}
The parameter gradient $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is computed efficiently via the backpropagation algorithm \cite{goodfellow2016deep}. Deep learning frameworks construct a directed acyclic graph (DAG) representing the sequence of mathematical operations. During the backward pass, the chain rule of differential calculus is applied in reverse topological order, accumulating sensitivities from the scalar loss backward to every trainable weight and bias.

\section{Why Neural Networks? Capabilities and Physical Limitations}
\label{sec:why_neural_networks}

\subsection{Capabilities and Universal Function Approximation}
\label{subsec:capabilities_universal}
Neural networks have achieved transformative breakthroughs across computational science and engineering due to three foundational properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Universal Approximation Theorem}: According to the classic theorems of Hornik et al. \cite{hornik1989multilayer} and Cybenko \cite{cybenko1989approximation}, an MLP with at least one hidden layer and continuous non-linear activation functions can approximate any continuous function on a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^d$ to arbitrary accuracy, provided sufficient neurons are available.
    \item \textbf{Meshless Continuous Representation}: Unlike traditional grid-based methods where solutions are defined exclusively at discrete nodes, neural networks act as continuous, differentiable functions $\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^m$ defined across the entire domain.
    \item \textbf{Hardware and Tensor Synergy}: Modern computational hardware—specifically GPUs and Tensor Processing Units (TPUs)—is engineered precisely for massively parallel dense matrix multiplications. Evaluating neural network forward and backward passes scales seamlessly on modern parallel architectures, decoupling inference cost from geometric mesh topology.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Critical Bottlenecks of Purely Data-Driven Machine Learning}
\label{subsec:black_box_limits}
Despite these formidable mathematical capabilities, deploying standard "black-box" neural networks to physical and fluid mechanical systems reveals critical vulnerabilities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Extreme Data Hunger and Scarcity}: Standard deep learning models rely on vast quantities of high-fidelity labeled training data. In industrial rheology and experimental fluid mechanics, acquiring dense volumetric velocity, stress, and pressure data via techniques such as Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) or laser Doppler anemometry is prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, or experimentally impossible.
    \item \textbf{Lack of Physical Grounding and Conservation Violations}: Purely data-driven neural networks optimize statistical correlation rather than physical causation. Consequently, their predictions routinely violate fundamental physical conservation laws, such as mass conservation ($\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u} \ne 0$) or linear momentum balance.
    \item \textbf{Catastrophic Extrapolation Failure}: While black-box networks interpolate reasonably well within their training envelope, they generalize poorly outside the training distribution, producing unphysical artifacts and numerical instability when exposed to altered Reynolds or Weissenberg numbers.
    \item \textbf{Overfitting to Sparse Measurements}: In the low-data regime, highly parameterized deep networks easily memorize sparse sensor noise, fitting individual data points with high accuracy while developing spurious, non-physical oscillations across unobserved regions.
    \item \textbf{Opacity and Lack of Interpretability}: The black-box nature of deep neural networks impedes rigorous engineering verification, precluding the extraction of interpretable governing parameters (such as viscosity or relaxation time).
\end{itemize}

These fundamental deficiencies motivated the development of Physics-Informed Neural Networks, which embed mathematical physics directly into the deep learning optimization cycle.

\section{The Physics-Informed Neural Network Paradigm}
\label{sec:pinn_paradigm}

The Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) paradigm, formalized by Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis \cite{raissi2019physics}, bridges the gap between classical numerical methods and deep learning by treating physical differential equations as an exact loss regularization mechanism.

\subsection{Physics-Constrained Loss Formulation}
\label{subsec:pinn_loss_formulation}
Consider a general non-linear differential operator $\mathcal{F}$ governing a continuous field $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})$ over an open spatial domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ bounded by $\partial \Omega$:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{F}[\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}); \boldsymbol{\lambda}] &= \boldsymbol{0} , \quad \boldsymbol{x} \in \Omega , \label{eq:general_pde_operator} \\
    \mathcal{B}[\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})] &= \boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}) , \quad \boldsymbol{x} \in \partial \Omega , \label{eq:general_bc_operator}
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ represents physical parameters (e.g., relaxation time, viscosity) and $\mathcal{B}$ denotes boundary differential operators.

In a PINN, the unknown solution $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})$ is approximated by an MLP $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta})$. Substituting $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ into the governing equations defines the differential residual:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \equiv \mathcal{F}[\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}); \boldsymbol{\lambda}] .
    \label{eq:pde_residual_def}
\end{equation}

The network parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ (and optionally the unknown physical parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$) are trained jointly by minimizing a composite multi-objective loss function:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = w_{\mathrm{data}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} + w_{\mathrm{bc}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} + w_{\mathrm{pde}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} ,
    \label{eq:composite_loss_pinn}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{data}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{data}}} \left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_i^{\mathrm{data}}) - \boldsymbol{u}_i^{\mathrm{data}} \right\|_2^2 , \label{eq:loss_data_comp} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{bc}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bc}}} \left\| \mathcal{B}[\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}})] - \boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}}) \right\|_2^2 , \label{eq:loss_bc_comp} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{pde}}} \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{pde}}} \left\| \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}}; \boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \right\|_2^2 . \label{eq:loss_pde_comp}
\end{align}
Here, $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_i^{\mathrm{data}} \}$ denotes sparse experimental or synthetic measurements, $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}} \}$ represents points sampled along the physical boundaries $\partial \Omega$, and $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}} \}$ designates unlabelled interior \textit{collocation points} scattered across $\Omega$.

\subsection{Deep Dive: Standard Supervised Training vs. PINN Optimization}
\label{subsec:deep_dive_training}
The operational differences between conventional deep learning training and physics-informed optimization are fundamental:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Loss Surface and Inductive Bias}: In conventional supervised training, the network optimizes an empirical risk function over a finite discrete set of input-output pairs $\mathcal{D} = \{ (\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{y}_i) \}_{i=1}^N$. Between adjacent training points, the network is mathematically unconstrained. In over-parameterized regimes, this lack of constraint allows the network to develop high-frequency oscillations that fit the sample points perfectly while completely failing on the underlying continuous function (overfitting).
    
    Conversely, in PINN training, the loss function incorporates the PDE residual $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}$ evaluated at tens of thousands of interior collocation points $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}} \}$. The differential operator $\mathcal{F}$ acts as an exact, infinite-dimensional \textit{inductive bias}. A network cannot form arbitrary interpolating curves through sparse data points without incurring massive penalties from spatial derivative mismatches (e.g., curvature, convective terms, or pressure gradients). Thus, \textbf{PINNs do not overfit} because the continuous physical laws constrain the entire functional space across the whole domain $\Omega$.

    \item \textbf{The Automatic Differentiation (AD) Analogy}:
    The foundational computational mechanism enabling PINNs is Automatic Differentiation (AD) \cite{baydin2018automatic}. In conventional deep learning, backpropagation applies AD to evaluate derivatives of the scalar loss with respect to network parameters:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Backpropagation (Classical DL):} \quad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}} .
    \end{equation}
    In PINNs, the exact same computational graph and reverse-mode accumulation chain are employed to evaluate derivatives with respect to the spatial input coordinates:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Coordinate AD (PINNs):} \quad \frac{\partial \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}}, \quad \frac{\partial^2 \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}^2} .
    \end{equation}
    As illustrated in Table~\ref{tab:differentiation_comparison}, coordinate AD computes exact analytical derivatives up to machine floating-point precision without requiring discrete difference stencils.

    \item \textbf{Meshless Formulation}:
    Traditional CFD solvers (FEM, FVM, FDM) require a spatial grid that discretizes the domain into cells or elements, necessitating element connectivity lists, edge neighbor tables, and assembled global stiffness matrices. Grid generation for complex geometries (such as the four-roll mill with narrow gaps) is computationally intensive and prone to element distortion, mesh skewness, and numerical diffusion.
    
    In sharp contrast, PINN collocation points $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}} \}$ are treated as an unorganized point cloud in $\mathbb{R}^d$. They require no topological connectivity, no cell volumes, and no adjacency relationships. Collocation points can be redistributed dynamically or concentrated locally in steep boundary layers without re-meshing overhead.
\end{enumerate}

    \centering
    \caption{Comparison of differentiation paradigms in scientific computing and deep learning.}
    \label{tab:differentiation_comparison}
    \begin{tabular}{lccc}

\section{Foundational Literature Benchmarks}
\label{sec:literature_benchmarks}

\subsection{Navier--Stokes Flow Past a Cylinder (Raissi et al., 2019)}
\label{subsec:cylinder_benchmark}
In their foundational 2019 publication, Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis \cite{raissi2019physics} demonstrated the capabilities of PINNs on the canonical benchmark of two-dimensional unsteady laminar flow past a circular cylinder at Reynolds number $Re = 100$, exhibiting vortex shedding (the periodic Von Kármán vortex street).

\subsubsection{Problem Formulation and Inverse Discovery}
The governing non-dimensional incompressible Navier--Stokes equations are:
\begin{align}
    u_t + \lambda_1 (u u_x + v u_y) &= -p_x + \lambda_2 (u_{xx} + u_{yy}) , \label{eq:cylinder_ns_u} \\
    v_t + \lambda_1 (u v_x + v v_y) &= -p_y + \lambda_2 (v_{xx} + v_{yy}) , \label{eq:cylinder_ns_v} \\
    u_x + v_y &= 0 , \label{eq:cylinder_cont}
\end{align}
where $\lambda_1$ represents the convective acceleration coefficient ($\lambda_1^{\mathrm{true}} = 1.0$) and $\lambda_2$ corresponds to the kinematic viscosity ($\lambda_2^{\mathrm{true}} = 1/Re = 0.01$).

To satisfy incompressibility identically, Raissi et al. introduced a continuous scalar stream function $\psi(t, x, y)$ such that:
\begin{equation}
    u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}, \qquad v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} .
    \label{eq:stream_function_cylinder}
\end{equation}
A single deep neural network with 9 fully-connected layers and 20 neurons per layer, activated by $\tanh$, was trained to predict the latent scalar fields $(\psi, p)$ from spatiotemporal coordinates $(t, x, y)$, while simultaneously estimating the unknown physical parameters $(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)$.

\subsubsection{Key Breakthroughs: Hidden Pressure and Noise Robustness}
Training was conducted using an ultra-sparse dataset of only $N = 5{,}000$ points (representing approximately $1\%$ of the space-time flow domain) optimized with 200{,}000 Adam iterations followed by 50{,}000 L-BFGS steps. This benchmark demonstrated two crucial breakthroughs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{"Hidden" Pressure Field Discovery}: Crucially, \textit{no pressure data were provided during training} ($N_{\mathrm{data}, p} = 0$). By leveraging the momentum equations~\eqref{eq:cylinder_ns_u}--\eqref{eq:cylinder_ns_v}, the network inferred the complete spatio-temporal pressure field $p(t, x, y)$ exclusively from velocity data and momentum residuals, matching the reference CFD pressure field up to an arbitrary hydrostatic gauge constant.
    \item \textbf{Inverse Parameter Identification under Noise}: The physical parameters were identified with remarkable accuracy:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Clean Data: $\lambda_1$ error of $0.08\%$, $\lambda_2$ error of $4.67\%$;
        \item $1\%$ Gaussian Noise: $\lambda_1$ error of $0.17\%$, $\lambda_2$ error of $5.70\%$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The ViscoelasticNet Framework (Thakur et al., 2024)}
\label{subsec:viscoelasticnet_benchmark}
Extending PINNs from Newtonian flows to complex viscoelastic fluids, Thakur, Raissi, and Ardekani \cite{thakur2024viscoelasticnet} introduced \textit{ViscoelasticNet}, published in the \textit{Journal of Non-Newtonian Fluid Mechanics}.

\subsubsection{The Rheological Bottleneck}
Viscoelastic fluids—such as biological fluids, polymer solutions, and molten plastics—exhibit memory effects, normal stress differences, and shear-thinning. Characterizing these flows presents severe experimental and computational bottlenecks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Experimental Limits}: While modern optical techniques (PIV) resolve kinematic velocity fields $\boldsymbol{u} = (u, v)$, non-intrusive measurement of the internal extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau} = (\tau_{xx}, \tau_{xy}, \tau_{yy})$ is practically impossible in general geometries.
    \item \textbf{Computational Breakdown (HWNP)}: Conventional FEM/FVM solvers suffer from numerical breakdown at high Weissenberg numbers ($Wi > 0.5$) due to hyperbolic stress advection, requiring artificial stabilization techniques (SUPG, log-conformation) that introduce non-physical numerical diffusion.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{The Virtual Rheometer Concept}
ViscoelasticNet overcomes these bottlenecks by formulating a physics-informed "virtual rheometer":
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latent Stress Discovery from Velocity Only}: Using exclusively sparse velocity observations $\boldsymbol{u}$, the framework infers the hidden polymeric extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ and pressure field $p$ throughout the flow domain.
    \item \textbf{Constitutive Model Selection and Parameter Discovery}: By formulating a unified constitutive equation encompassing Oldroyd-B, linear PTT, and Giesekus models:
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{\tau} + \lambda \overset{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{\tau}} + \frac{\alpha \lambda}{\mu_p} (\boldsymbol{\tau} \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau}) + \frac{\varepsilon \lambda}{\mu_p} \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau}) \boldsymbol{\tau} = 2\mu_p \boldsymbol{D} ,
        \label{eq:unified_constitutive_thakur}
    \end{equation}
    the network simultaneously identifies the relaxation time $\lambda$, polymeric viscosity $\mu_p$, and non-linear mobility parameters ($\alpha, \varepsilon$), classifying the constitutive model governing the uncharacterized fluid.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Network Architecture and Two-Stage Training}: ViscoelasticNet deploys three dedicated neural networks (Velocity Net, Stress Net, Pressure Net). Optimization is decoupled into two sequential stages:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Stage 1 (Stress and Rheology)}: Joint optimization of velocity and stress networks by minimizing velocity mismatch and constitutive residuals, learning the rheological parameters $(\lambda, \mu_p, \alpha, \varepsilon)$.
        \item \textit{Stage 2 (Pressure and Viscosity)}: The stress network is frozen, and the pressure network is trained alongside solvent viscosity $\mu_s$ by minimizing momentum residuals.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Validation Benchmarks}: Evaluated on 2D sinusoidal stenosis flow and cross-slot flow, ViscoelasticNet achieved relative stress errors below $5\%$ and demonstrated resilience to up to $10\%$ Gaussian noise on velocity fields and $25\%$ noise on boundary conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Gap and Dissertation Objectives}
\label{sec:literature_gap_objectives}

Despite the breakthrough achievements of ViscoelasticNet, critical literature gaps persist:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Benchmark Simplicity vs. Geometric Complexity}: Existing viscoelastic PINN studies have focused predominantly on simple channels, idealized stenosis geometries, or cross-slots. Extending PINNs to enclosed, recirculating domains featuring rotating boundaries and intense extensional stagnation kinematics—such as the Four-Roll Mill—remains an open research frontier.
    \item \textbf{Steady-State Stream Function Formulation}: Unlike unsteady channel formulations, steady enclosed flows with zero net flux demand exact mass conservation without relying on soft penalty terms. In this dissertation, we formulate an exact stream function $\psi$ ($u = \partial \psi / \partial y, v = -\partial \psi / \partial x$), guaranteeing $\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u} \equiv 0$ analytically.
    \item \textbf{Substantially Extended Parameter Bounds}: ViscoelasticNet evaluated narrow parameter spaces ($\lambda \le 0.15\,\mathrm{s}$, $\mu_p \le 0.025\,\mathrm{Pa\cdot s}$, $\alpha \le 0.20$). In this work, the parameter domain is expanded by up to an order of magnitude: $\lambda$ up to $1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ ($10\times$), $\mu_p$ up to $0.98\,\mathrm{Pa\cdot s}$ ($40\times$), and mobility $\alpha$ up to $0.50$.
    \item \textbf{Alignment with Recent Literature (2026)}: The recent preprint by Matos, Fernandes, and Alves \cite{matos2026simulation} explores steady viscoelastic creeping flows with PINNs, underscoring the high timeliness and active development of physics-informed frameworks in complex rheology.
\end{enumerate}

Building upon these foundations, this dissertation develops a staged, multi-head physics-informed neural network framework tailored to the four-roll mill geometry, resolving both direct hydrodynamic fields and inverse constitutive parameter discovery across linear and non-linear viscoelastic regimes.
