\chapter{Physics-Informed Neural Networks in Complex Rheology}
\label{chap:pinn_literature}

In this chapter, we present the theoretical and computational foundations of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and their application to complex, viscoelastic fluid mechanics. The discussion is structured logically into five main sections. First, we outline the fundamental principles of deep learning, covering artificial neurons, multi-layer perceptrons, activation functions, and gradient-based optimization algorithms. Second, we examine why neural networks represent a compelling paradigm for scientific computing, while critically assessing the structural limitations of purely data-driven black-box architectures. Third, we formalize the PINN framework, providing an in-depth comparative analysis between conventional supervised training and physics-informed optimization, detailing how Automatic Differentiation (AD) enables an exact, mesh-free formulation that naturally suppresses overfitting. 
Fourth, we review benchmarks from the literature, focusing on the foundational Navier--Stokes cylinder flow by Raissi et al. \cite{raissi2019physics} and the ViscoelasticNet framework by Thakur et al. \cite{thakur2024viscoelasticnet}. Finally, we identify the literature gap and delineate the specific methodological contributions of this dissertation.

% 3.1 FUNDAMENTALS OF DEEP LEARNING

\section{Fundamentals of Deep Learning}
\label{sec:dl_fundamentals}

Deep learning is a subfield of machine learning based on artificial neural networks that learn hierarchical representations of continuous mathematical functions through many layers of non-linear transformations \cite{goodfellow2016deep}.

\subsection{The Artificial Neuron and Non-Linear Activation Functions}
\label{subsec:perceptron_activations}
The elementary computational unit of a neural network is the artificial neuron (perceptron). Given an input vector $\boldsymbol{x}$, the neuron computes an affine linear transformation followed by an element-wise non-linear scalar mapping:
\begin{equation}
    z = \boldsymbol{w}^T \boldsymbol{x} + b , \qquad a = \sigma(z) ,
    \label{eq:single_neuron}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{w}$ denotes the weight vector, $b$ represents the scalar bias, and $\sigma(z)$ is the activation function. 
The activation function $\sigma$ imparts non-linearity to the network, enabling it to approximate complex, non-linear physical operators.

In conventional deep learning tasks such as computer vision or classification, piecewise-linear activations—most notably the Rectified Linear Unit ($\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$)—are widely adopted due to their low computational cost and non-saturating gradients. In physics-informed scientific machine learning, however, the choice of activation function is strictly governed by the differential nature of the target boundary value problem. Because governing fluid mechanical residuals involve spatial derivatives of the state fields up to second or higher order (such as the Laplacian $\nabla^2 \boldsymbol{u}$ in linear momentum or stress divergence $\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau}$), the network must satisfy $\sigma \in \mathcal{C}^k$ with $k \ge 2$. Standard piecewise-linear activations have a first derivative that is discontinuous (the Heaviside step function) and a second derivative that is identically zero almost everywhere ($\text{ReLU}''(z) \equiv 0$ for $z \neq 0$). Consequently, evaluating a ReLU network inside second-order differential operators yields trivial zero evaluations of diffusive and viscous terms, entirely precluding the backpropagation of PDE residual gradients.

Therefore, PINN architectures strictly require smooth, higher-order differentiable activations ($\mathcal{C}^\infty$). In this work, all network representations employ the Sigmoid Linear Unit (SiLU, also known as Swish) \cite{goodfellow2016deep}:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SiLU}(z) = z \cdot \text{sigmoid}(z) = \frac{z}{1 + e^{-z}} .
    \label{eq:silu_activation}
\end{equation}
The SiLU activation function is strictly smooth ($\mathcal{C}^\infty$), non-monotonic, self-gated, and possesses continuous second and third derivatives throughout $\mathbb{R}$. Unlike classic bounded sigmoidal functions (such as $\tanh$) that saturate exponentially toward asymptotic limits ($\pm 1$) for large inputs, SiLU maintains active gradient flow in the positive regime ($\sigma'(z) \to 1$ as $z \to \infty$). This non-saturating property is advantageous in viscoelastic flow modeling, where intense localized shear layers and extensional stress cusps near roller surfaces generate large localized field values that would otherwise induce gradient vanishing.

\subsection{Multi-Layer Perceptron Architecture}
\label{subsec:mlp_architecture}
By arranging neurons into sequential layers, a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) is constructed. An $L$-layer deep neural network maps an input coordinate vector $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ to an output field prediction $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}$ through the composition:
\begin{align}
    \boldsymbol{h}^{(0)} &= \boldsymbol{x} , \nonumber \\
    \boldsymbol{h}^{(l)} &= \sigma \left( \boldsymbol{W}^{(l)} \boldsymbol{h}^{(l-1)} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(l)} \right) , \quad \text{for } l = 1, \dots, L-1 , \label{eq:mlp_layers} \\
    \hat{\boldsymbol{u}} &= \boldsymbol{W}^{(L)} \boldsymbol{h}^{(L-1)} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(L)} , \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol{W}^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_l \times N_{l-1}}$ and $\boldsymbol{b}^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_l}$ denote the weight matrix and bias vector of the $l$-th hidden layer, with $N_l$ representing the layer width. The total collection of trainable parameters is denoted by $\boldsymbol{\theta} = \{ \boldsymbol{W}^{(l)}, \boldsymbol{b}^{(l)} \}_{l=1}^L$. The final output layer is strictly linear to guarantee an unconstrained dynamic range for continuous physical quantities.

\subsubsection{Weight Initialization: Glorot Scheme and Output Zero-Initialization}
\label{subsubsec:weight_initialization}
To maintain stable signal variance throughout deep architectures and avoid vanishing or exploding gradients during early iterations, hidden layer weights are initialized using the Xavier/Glorot Normal distribution \cite{goodfellow2016deep}:
\begin{equation}
    W_{ij}^{(l)} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \, \frac{2}{N_{l-1} + N_l}\right) , \qquad b_i^{(l)} = 0 .
    \label{eq:glorot_initialization}
\end{equation}
For physical outputs representing extra-stress tensor components $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ and pressure $p$, a strategic zero-initialization of the final layer is adopted ($\boldsymbol{W}^{(L)} = \boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{b}^{(L)} = \boldsymbol{0}$). This formulation is grounded in two key physical and numerical considerations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Thermodynamic Rest-State Baseline}: In continuum mechanics, a fluid at thermodynamic rest possesses zero extra-stress ($\boldsymbol{\tau} = \boldsymbol{0}$). Zero-initialization guarantees that at step zero ($t = 0$), the network output corresponds exactly to the unperturbed physical baseline, avoiding arbitrary initial kinematic biases.
    \item \textbf{Suppression of Non-Linear Residual Shocks}: If output weights were initialized randomly, initial stress predictions would exhibit high-frequency spatial noise across the domain. Evaluating these random fluctuations inside non-linear constitutive equations (which contain quadratic tensor products $\boldsymbol{\tau} \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau}$ and convective gradients $\boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla \boldsymbol{\tau}$) causes initial residual losses to spike violently ($10^5 - 10^8$), producing massive gradient shocks that destabilize hidden layer weights before physical features can be resolved. Initializing the output layer to zero guarantees bounded initial PDE residuals, allowing stress distributions to develop smoothly and progressively from boundary velocity inputs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Loss Functions and Optimization Algorithms}
\label{subsec:optimization_solvers}
Training an MLP consists of solving an unconstrained non-convex optimization problem over the parameter space:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta}^* = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) ,
    \label{eq:opt_problem}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ measures the discrepancy between predicted and target quantities, typically evaluated via the Mean Squared Error (MSE):
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) - \boldsymbol{u}_i^{\mathrm{target}} \right\|_2^2 .
    \label{eq:mse_loss}
\end{equation}

Optimization is performed using gradient-based algorithms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{First-Order Stochastic Gradient Descent (Adam)}: The Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) optimizer \cite{kingma2014adam} dynamically adjusts individual learning rates for each parameter by computing exponentially decaying moving averages of past gradients ($m_t$) and squared gradients ($v_t$):
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \epsilon} \hat{m}_t ,
    \end{equation}
    where $\eta$ is the learning rate. To balance rapid exploration with asymptotic refinement, $\eta$ is scheduled using a Cosine Annealing decay:
    \begin{equation}
        \eta_t = \eta_{\min} + \frac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min}) \left( 1 + \cos\left( \frac{\pi t}{T_{\max}} \right) \right) .
        \label{eq:cosine_annealing}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Second-Order Quasi-Newton Optimization (L-BFGS)}: While Adam navigates complex, non-convex loss landscapes efficiently, it exhibits slow convergence near shallow minima. The Limited-memory Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno (L-BFGS) algorithm approximates the inverse Hessian matrix using past gradient evaluations, achieving super-linear convergence. In physics-informed settings, L-BFGS paired with a Strong Wolfe line search is essential for driving PDE residuals to scientific precision ($10^{-5} - 10^{-7}$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Backpropagation and Computational Graphs}
\label{subsec:backpropagation}
The parameter gradient $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is computed efficiently via the backpropagation algorithm \cite{goodfellow2016deep}, which implements reverse-mode automatic differentiation on a directed acyclic graph (DAG). 

Let $\boldsymbol{z}^{(l)} = \boldsymbol{W}^{(l)} \boldsymbol{h}^{(l-1)} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(l)}$ denote the pre-activation linear vector at layer $l$, such that $\boldsymbol{h}^{(l)} = \sigma(\boldsymbol{z}^{(l)})$. Defining the layer-wise sensitivity vector $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ as:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} \equiv \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{z}^{(l)}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_l} ,
    \label{eq:sensitivity_vector}
\end{equation}
the backward pass begins at the linear output layer $L$ where $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}} = \boldsymbol{z}^{(L)}$:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}} .
\end{equation}
Applying the multivariate chain rule in reverse topological order yields the recursive backpropagation formula for hidden layers $l = L-1, \dots, 1$:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} = \left[ (\boldsymbol{W}^{(l+1)})^T \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)} \right] \odot \sigma'(\boldsymbol{z}^{(l)}) ,
    \label{eq:backprop_recursion}
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes the element-wise Hadamard product and $\sigma'$ is the first derivative of the activation function. Once the sensitivities $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ are evaluated, the exact parameter gradients with respect to weights and biases are given by:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{W}^{(l)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} (\boldsymbol{h}^{(l-1)})^T , \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{b}^{(l)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} .
    \label{eq:param_gradients}
\end{equation}

Evaluating the parameter gradient via naive numerical perturbation would require $\mathcal{O}(P)$ forward passes, leading to an intractable $\mathcal{O}(P^2)$ computational complexity for a network with $P$ parameters. In contrast, reverse-mode accumulation evaluates all sensitivities across the computational graph in a single backward sweep, achieving exact gradients at a cost proportional to a single forward evaluation ($\mathcal{O}(P)$ complexity).

\section{Why Neural Networks? Capabilities and Physical Limitations}
\label{sec:why_neural_networks}

\subsection{Capabilities and Universal Function Approximation}
\label{subsec:capabilities_universal}
Neural networks offer attractive mathematical and computational properties for scientific computing:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Universal Approximation Property}: According to the foundational theorems of Hornik et al. \cite{hornik1989multilayer} and Cybenko \cite{cybenko1989approximation}, a feedforward neural network with at least one hidden layer and continuous non-linear activation functions can approximate any continuous function on a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^d$ to arbitrary accuracy, given sufficient network width. While this theorem guarantees the representational capacity of neural networks for continuous physical fields, it is non-constructive; it does not ensure that unconstrained empirical loss optimization will discover physically consistent parameters from sparse data.
    \item \textbf{Meshless Continuous Representation}: Unlike grid-based numerical methods where state variables exist exclusively at discrete vertices or cell centers, neural networks define continuous, differentiable field mappings over the entire spatial domain.
    \item \textbf{Hardware and Tensor Acceleration}: Neural network evaluations rely primarily on dense matrix operations that map directly onto massively parallel accelerator architectures (GPUs and TPUs), decoupling spatial evaluation cost from geometric grid connectivity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Critical Bottlenecks of Purely Data-Driven Machine Learning}
\label{subsec:black_box_limits}
Despite their high expressivity, deploying purely data-driven, black-box neural networks to fluid dynamics and non-Newtonian mechanics reveals severe limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data Scarcity in Experimental Rheology}: Deep neural networks typically require voluminous labeled datasets. In experimental fluid mechanics and rheology, acquiring dense volumetric velocity, stress, and pressure data via techniques such as Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) or laser Doppler anemometry is challenging, noisy, or experimentally impossible—particularly for internal stress components.
    \item \textbf{Violation of Physical Conservation Laws}: Purely data-driven networks optimize statistical correlations over discrete training sets without respecting physical principles. Consequently, their predictions routinely violate mass conservation ($\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u} \neq 0$) and linear momentum balance, producing unphysical flow features and failing when evaluated outside the narrow envelope of training conditions.
    \item \textbf{Overfitting to Sparse and Noisy Measurements}: In low-data regimes, over-parameterized models readily fit experimental noise or sparse sensor readings with minimal training error, but develop high-frequency, non-physical oscillations across unmonitored subdomains.
    \item \textbf{Black-Box Opacity and Lack of Parameter Discoverability}: Standard architectures lack physical interpretability and cannot infer underlying governing material parameters—such as fluid relaxation times or polymeric viscosities—from observed flow kinematics.
\end{itemize}

These fundamental shortcomings motivated the development of Physics-Informed Neural Networks, which embed mathematical governing equations directly into the loss function.

\section{The Physics-Informed Neural Network Paradigm}
\label{sec:pinn_paradigm}

The Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) framework, formalized by Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis \cite{raissi2019physics}, integrates differential equations directly into deep learning optimization, penalizing deviations from physical laws without relying on spatial discretization meshes.

\subsection{Physics-Constrained Loss Formulation}
\label{subsec:pinn_loss_formulation}
Consider a general non-linear differential operator $\mathcal{F}$ governing a continuous field $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})$ over an open spatial domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ bounded by $\partial \Omega$:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{F}[\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}); \boldsymbol{\lambda}] &= \boldsymbol{0} , \quad \boldsymbol{x} \in \Omega , \label{eq:general_pde_operator} \\
    \mathcal{B}[\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})] &= \boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}) , \quad \boldsymbol{x} \in \partial \Omega , \label{eq:general_bc_operator}
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ represents physical parameters (e.g., relaxation time, viscosity) and $\mathcal{B}$ denotes boundary differential operators.

In a PINN, the unknown solution $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})$ is approximated by an MLP $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta})$. Substituting $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ into the governing equations defines the differential residual:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \equiv \mathcal{F}[\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}); \boldsymbol{\lambda}] .
    \label{eq:pde_residual_def}
\end{equation}

The network parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ (and optionally the unknown physical parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$) are trained jointly by minimizing a composite multi-objective loss function:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = w_{\mathrm{data}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} + w_{\mathrm{bc}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} + w_{\mathrm{pde}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} ,
    \label{eq:composite_loss_pinn}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{data}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{data}}} \left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_i^{\mathrm{data}}) - \boldsymbol{u}_i^{\mathrm{data}} \right\|_2^2 , \label{eq:loss_data_comp} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{bc}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bc}}} \left\| \mathcal{B}[\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}})] - \boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}}) \right\|_2^2 , \label{eq:loss_bc_comp} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{pde}}} \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{pde}}} \left\| \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}}; \boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \right\|_2^2 . \label{eq:loss_pde_comp}
\end{align}
Here, $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_i^{\mathrm{data}} \}$ denotes sparse experimental or synthetic measurements, $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}} \}$ represents points sampled along the physical boundaries $\partial \Omega$, and $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}} \}$ designates unlabelled interior \textit{collocation points} scattered across $\Omega$.

\subsection{Deep Dive: Standard Supervised Training vs. PINN Optimization}
\label{subsec:deep_dive_training}
The operational differences between conventional deep learning training and physics-informed optimization are fundamental:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Loss Surface and Inductive Bias}: In conventional supervised training, the network optimizes an empirical risk function over a finite discrete set of input-output pairs $\mathcal{D} = \{ (\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{y}_i) \}_{i=1}^N$. Between adjacent training points, the network is mathematically unconstrained. In over-parameterized regimes, this lack of constraint allows the network to develop high-frequency oscillations that fit the sample points perfectly while completely failing on the underlying continuous function (overfitting).
    
    Conversely, in PINN training, the loss function incorporates the PDE residual $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}$ evaluated at tens of thousands of interior collocation points $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}} \}$. The differential operator $\mathcal{F}$ acts as an exact, infinite-dimensional \textit{inductive bias}. A network cannot form arbitrary interpolating curves through sparse data points without incurring massive penalties from spatial derivative mismatches (e.g., curvature, convective terms, or pressure gradients). Thus, \textbf{PINNs do not overfit} because the continuous physical laws constrain the entire functional space across the whole domain $\Omega$.

    \item \textbf{The Automatic Differentiation (AD) Analogy}:
    The foundational computational mechanism enabling PINNs is Automatic Differentiation (AD) \cite{baydin2018automatic}. In conventional deep learning, backpropagation applies AD to evaluate derivatives of the scalar loss with respect to network parameters:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Backpropagation (Classical DL):} \quad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}} .
    \end{equation}
    In PINNs, the exact same computational graph and reverse-mode accumulation chain are employed to evaluate derivatives with respect to the spatial input coordinates:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Coordinate AD (PINNs):} \quad \frac{\partial \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}}, \quad \frac{\partial^2 \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}^2} .
    \end{equation}
    As illustrated in Table~\ref{tab:differentiation_comparison}, coordinate AD computes exact analytical derivatives up to machine floating-point precision without requiring discrete difference stencils.

    \item \textbf{Meshless Formulation}:
    Traditional CFD solvers (FEM, FVM, FDM) require a spatial grid that discretizes the domain into cells or elements, necessitating element connectivity lists, edge neighbor tables, and assembled global stiffness matrices. Grid generation for complex geometries (such as the four-roll mill with narrow gaps) is computationally intensive and prone to element distortion, mesh skewness, and numerical diffusion.
    
    In sharp contrast, PINN collocation points $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}} \}$ are treated as an unorganized point cloud in $\mathbb{R}^d$. They require no topological connectivity, no cell volumes, and no adjacency relationships. Collocation points can be redistributed dynamically or concentrated locally in steep boundary layers without re-meshing overhead.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Foundational Literature Benchmarks}
\label{sec:literature_benchmarks}

\subsection{Navier--Stokes Flow Past a Cylinder (Raissi et al., 2019)}
\label{subsec:cylinder_benchmark}
In their foundational study, Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis \cite{raissi2019physics} demonstrated the capabilities of PINNs on the canonical benchmark of two-dimensional unsteady laminar flow past a circular cylinder at Reynolds number $Re = 100$, characterized by periodic vortex shedding (the Von Kármán vortex street).

\subsubsection{Problem Formulation and Inverse Discovery}
The governing non-dimensional incompressible Navier--Stokes equations are formulated as:
\begin{align}
    u_t + \lambda_1 (u u_x + v u_y) &= -p_x + \lambda_2 (u_{xx} + u_{yy}) , \label{eq:cylinder_ns_u} \\
    v_t + \lambda_1 (u v_x + v v_y) &= -p_y + \lambda_2 (v_{xx} + v_{yy}) , \label{eq:cylinder_ns_v} \\
    u_x + v_y &= 0 , \label{eq:cylinder_cont}
\end{align}
where $\lambda_1$ denotes the convective acceleration coefficient ($\lambda_1^{\mathrm{true}} = 1.0$) and $\lambda_2$ corresponds to the kinematic viscosity ($\lambda_2^{\mathrm{true}} = 1/Re = 0.01$).

To satisfy the incompressibility condition~\eqref{eq:cylinder_cont} identically, Raissi et al. adopted the scalar stream function formulation $\psi(t, x, y)$ established in Chapter~\ref{chap:fluidodynamic_background} (Eq.~\eqref{eq:stream_function_def}):
\begin{equation}
    u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}, \qquad v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} .
    \label{eq:stream_function_cylinder}
\end{equation}
A single neural network with 9 fully-connected hidden layers and 20 neurons per layer was employed to predict the latent scalar fields $(\psi, p)$ from coordinates $(t, x, y)$, while simultaneously estimating the unknown physical coefficients $(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)$. In this classic setup, neurons were activated using the hyperbolic tangent function:
\begin{equation}
    \tanh(z) = \frac{e^z - e^{-z}}{e^z + e^{-z}} ,
    \label{eq:tanh_activation}
\end{equation}
which is smooth and symmetric around zero, providing continuous spatial derivatives.

\subsubsection{Latent Field Discovery and Parameter Identification}
The network was trained on an ultra-sparse dataset of $N = 5{,}000$ spatio-temporal velocity points (corresponding to approximately $1\%$ of the computational space-time volume) using 200{,}000 Adam iterations followed by 50{,}000 L-BFGS steps. This benchmark illustrated two practical capabilities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latent Pressure Field Discovery}: No pressure measurements were provided during training ($N_{\mathrm{data}, p} = 0$). By enforcing the momentum residuals~\eqref{eq:cylinder_ns_u}--\eqref{eq:cylinder_ns_v}, the network reconstructed the complete spatio-temporal pressure field $p(t, x, y)$ solely from velocity data and governing physical laws, recovering the reference CFD pressure distribution up to an arbitrary hydrostatic reference constant.
    \item \textbf{Parameter Identification Under Noise}: The physical coefficients were identified with high accuracy. For clean velocity data, the relative estimation errors were $0.08\%$ for $\lambda_1$ and $4.67\%$ for $\lambda_2$. When $1\%$ uncorrelated Gaussian noise was added to the velocity data, the errors remained stable at $0.17\%$ for $\lambda_1$ and $5.70\%$ for $\lambda_2$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The ViscoelasticNet Framework (Thakur et al., 2024)}
\label{subsec:viscoelasticnet_benchmark}
Extending PINNs from Newtonian flows to complex non-Newtonian media, Thakur, Raissi, and Ardekani \cite{thakur2024viscoelasticnet} introduced \textit{ViscoelasticNet}, published in the \textit{Journal of Non-Newtonian Fluid Mechanics}.

\subsubsection{The Rheological Bottleneck}
Viscoelastic fluids exhibit memory effects, normal stress differences, and shear-dependent viscosities. Investigating these systems numerically and experimentally presents distinct challenges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Experimental Limits}: While optical diagnostics (e.g., PIV) capture the velocity field $\boldsymbol{u}$, non-intrusive measurements of the internal extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ are generally inaccessible in arbitrary flow geometries.
    \item \textbf{Numerical Instability (HWNP)}: Traditional grid-based methods (FEM, FVM) suffer from loss of convergence at high Weissenberg numbers ($Wi > 0.5$) due to hyperbolic stress advection and steep stress gradients near stagnation zones, often requiring numerical diffusion (e.g., SUPG) to maintain stability.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{The Virtual Rheometer Concept}
ViscoelasticNet addresses these challenges by formulating a physics-informed "virtual rheometer":
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latent Stress Inference from Velocity}: Using solely sparse velocity observations $\boldsymbol{u}$, the framework infers the hidden extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ and pressure field $p$ throughout the flow domain.
    \item \textbf{Constitutive Model Selection and Parameter Discovery}: By formulating a unified constitutive equation encompassing Oldroyd-B, linear PTT, and Giesekus models:
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{\tau} + \lambda \overset{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{\tau}} + \frac{\alpha \lambda}{\mu_p} (\boldsymbol{\tau} \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau}) + \frac{\varepsilon \lambda}{\mu_p} \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau}) \boldsymbol{\tau} = 2\mu_p \boldsymbol{D} ,
        \label{eq:unified_constitutive_thakur}
    \end{equation}
    the network simultaneously identifies the relaxation time $\lambda$, polymeric viscosity $\mu_p$, and non-linear mobility parameters ($\alpha, \varepsilon$), characterizing the constitutive behavior of the fluid.
    \item \textbf{Decoupled Multi-Network Architecture}: ViscoelasticNet deploys three distinct sub-networks (Velocity, Stress, and Pressure Nets). Optimization is structured into two sequential stages:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Stage 1 (Stress and Rheology)}: Velocity and stress networks are optimized jointly by minimizing velocity data mismatch and constitutive residuals, identifying rheological parameters $(\lambda, \mu_p, \alpha, \varepsilon)$.
        \item \textit{Stage 2 (Pressure and Viscosity)}: The stress network is frozen, and the pressure network is optimized alongside solvent viscosity $\mu_s$ by minimizing momentum residuals.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Validation Benchmarks}: On 2D planar channels (sinusoidal stenosis and cross-slot geometries), ViscoelasticNet achieved relative stress errors below $5\%$ and demonstrated resilience to noise levels up to $10\%$ on velocity observations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Gap and Dissertation Objectives}
\label{sec:literature_gap_objectives}

The formulation of physics-informed neural networks for non-Newtonian flows addresses two distinct levels of technological and scientific gaps: the fundamental limitations of classical numerical grid-based solvers, and the specific domain constraints that remain unaddressed in existing viscoelastic PINN frameworks.

\subsection{Why PINNs for Viscoelastic Flows in Complex Geometries}
\label{subsec:why_pinns_complex_rheology}
In complex polymeric flows, traditional grid-based computational fluid dynamics methods (Finite Element and Finite Volume Methods) encounter profound numerical bottlenecks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The High Weissenberg Number Problem (HWNP)}: At elevated elasticity, the hyperbolic constitutive advection of macromolecular stress creates exponential boundary layers and steep stress gradients near wall boundaries and stagnation points. Standard discretization schemes suffer from severe loss of convergence and numerical breakdown.
    \item \textbf{Mesh Distortion and Artificial Diffusion}: While numerical stabilization methods (such as SUPG and the log-conformation transformation) mitigate numerical breakdown, they introduce artificial numerical diffusion and demand intensive, geometrically conforming mesh refinement around rotating boundaries and narrow gaps.
    \item \textbf{Intractable Inverse Formulation}: Conventional CFD codes are designed strictly as forward solvers; extracting unknown rheological properties or reconstructing latent stress distributions from experimental velocity measurements requires costly iterative outer-loop optimization.
\end{enumerate}
By reformulating flow simulation as a continuous, mesh-free optimization problem governed by Automatic Differentiation, PINNs bypass element distortion, remain robust to geometric constraints, and naturally unify forward state prediction with inverse parameter identification.

\subsection{Advancing Beyond ViscoelasticNet: Specific Dissertation Contributions}
\label{subsec:gap_vs_viscoelasticnet}
While the ViscoelasticNet framework of Thakur et al. \cite{thakur2024viscoelasticnet} demonstrated the feasibility of PINNs for viscoelastic stress discovery, its application was confined to open channel domains (stenosis and cross-slot geometries) under restricted conditions. This dissertation advances the state of the art through three primary methodological contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Enclosed Domain with Mixed Kinematics (Four-Roll Mill)}: Unlike open channel flows governed by net pressure drops, the four-roll mill constitutes an enclosed recirculating cavity with zero net flux, driven by rotating cylinder boundaries. This geometry features a pure extensional hyperbolic stagnation point at the cavity center coupled with intense localized shear layers adjacent to the roller surfaces.
    \item \textbf{Exact Mass Conservation via Stream Function}: ViscoelasticNet utilized primitive velocity variables $(u, v)$ and enforced incompressibility as a soft penalty loss term ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{div}}$). In enclosed recirculating flows, soft divergence constraints allow gradual mass leakage. Here, we adopt the scalar stream function formulation $\psi$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:stream_function_def}), guaranteeing $\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u} \equiv 0$ analytically and eliminating the continuity residual entirely.
    \item \textbf{Substantially Extended Rheological Bounds}: Existing viscoelastic PINN studies examined restricted parameter spaces ($\lambda \le 0.15\,\mathrm{s}$, $\mu_p \le 0.025\,\mathrm{Pa\cdot s}$, $\alpha \le 0.20$). In this work, the rheological domain is extended up to an order of magnitude higher ($\lambda$ up to $1.0\,\mathrm{s}$, $\mu_p$ up to $0.98\,\mathrm{Pa\cdot s}$, and mobility $\alpha$ up to $0.50$), evaluating model robustness across pronounced non-linear regimes.
\end{enumerate}

The focus on steady-state creeping flows with physics-informed networks aligns closely with the most recent developments in computational rheology, notably the work of Matos, Fernandes, and Alves \cite{matos2026simulation}, corroborating that physics-informed formulations represent an active and timely frontier for non-Newtonian flow modeling.

Building upon these foundations, this dissertation develops a staged, multi-head physics-informed neural network framework tailored to the four-roll mill geometry, resolving both direct hydrodynamic fields and inverse constitutive parameter discovery across linear and non-linear viscoelastic regimes.
