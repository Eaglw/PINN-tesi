\chapter{Physics-Informed Neural Networks in Complex Rheology}
\label{chap:pinn_literature}

Feedforward deep networks, commonly known as Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs),
represent the quintessential architecture of deep learning \cite{goodfellow2016deep}.
Conceptually, an MLP is a parametric function defined by composing together a
sequence of simpler parametric functions. Each of these constituent functions has
multiple inputs and outputs, and in neural network terminology is called a
\textit{layer}. The scalar outputs computed within each layer are referred to as
\textit{units} or \textit{features}. Although each unit performs a relatively simple
mathematical mapping of its inputs, chaining them in succession allows the network as
a whole to express continuous functions of arbitrary complexity.

The structural dimensions of a network are conventionally characterized by its
\textit{width}—the number of units in a given layer—and its \textit{depth}—the total
number of layers. Having significant width and depth provides deep architectures with
remarkable representational power, making them the foundational technology behind
modern machine learning applications.

To appreciate the paradigm shift introduced by deep neural networks, it is useful to
contrast them with traditional machine learning algorithms, such as linear
regression, logistic classifiers, and kernel methods. Classical algorithms operate by
applying a linear transformation to a fixed set of features. These methods can indeed
learn non-linear behaviors, but their non-linearity is rigid: it is non-linear only
in the original input space, while remaining strictly linear in a feature space that
must be pre-defined and engineered manually by human experts. Neural networks
fundamentally overcome this limitation by automating the design of features. Rather
than relying on manual feature engineering, an MLP learns the optimal representations
directly from data.

In this architecture, the layers between the inputs and the final outputs are termed
\textit{hidden layers}. They are called hidden because their internal values are not
specified or observed in the training data; instead, the optimization algorithm must
autonomously determine how to organize these intermediate representations to solve
the target problem. By transforming inputs through successive hidden layers, neural
networks can approximate complex continuous relationships.

In this chapter, we explore how these feedforward architectures form the
computational core of scientific machine learning. We begin by reviewing the
fundamentals of neural networks and their optimization. We then examine why
continuous neural representations are compelling for physical modeling, while
discussing the critical limitations of purely data-driven black-box approaches. This
analysis naturally motivates the Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) paradigm,
followed by a review of foundational literature benchmarks and the specific research
objectives of this dissertation in complex viscoelastic flows.

\section{Fundamentals of Deep Learning}
\label{sec:dl_fundamentals}

Deep learning algorithms approximate continuous mathematical functions through
multi-layer architectures that learn internal representations from data
\cite{goodfellow2016deep}. In this section, we present the essential building blocks
of these networks, focusing on their mathematical structure, smooth activation
functions, optimization algorithms, and gradient evaluation.

\subsection{The Artificial Neuron and Non-Linear Activation Functions}
\label{subsec:perceptron_activations}

The elementary processing unit of a neural network is the artificial neuron. Given an
input vector $\boldsymbol{x}$, a neuron computes an affine combination of the inputs
followed by a scalar non-linear mapping:
\begin{equation}
    z = \boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x} + b , \qquad a = \sigma(z) ,
    \label{eq:single_neuron}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{w}$ is the weight vector, $b$ is a scalar bias, and
$\sigma(\cdot)$ is the activation function. The activation function introduces
non-linearity into the model. Without non-linear activations, composing multiple
linear layers would simply collapse mathematically into another single linear
mapping, eliminating the expressive advantage of deep architectures.

In standard deep learning applications such as image recognition, piecewise-linear
activations—most notably the Rectified Linear Unit ($\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0,
z)$)—are widely adopted due to their computational simplicity. However, in scientific
computing and fluid mechanics, the network's outputs represent continuous physical
fields governed by partial differential equations (PDEs), such as velocities,
pressures, and stresses. These governing laws involve spatial derivatives of first,
second, or higher order (such as viscous laplacians $\nabla^2 \boldsymbol{u}$ or
stress divergences $\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau}$).

Because the first derivative of ReLU is discontinuous and its second derivative is
zero almost everywhere ($\text{ReLU}''(z) \equiv 0$), evaluating a ReLU network
inside differential equations would yield zero for diffusive and curvature terms. For
this reason, physics-informed models strictly require smooth, infinitely
differentiable activation functions ($\mathcal{C}^\infty$). In this work, we employ
the Sigmoid Linear Unit (SiLU, also known as Swish) \cite{goodfellow2016deep}:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SiLU}(z) = \frac{z}{1 + e^{-z}} .
    \label{eq:silu_activation}
\end{equation}
The SiLU activation is smooth, non-monotonic, and self-gated. Unlike classical
functions such as the hyperbolic tangent ($\tanh$) that saturate toward constant
limits for large inputs, SiLU remains active in the positive regime. This property
helps maintain healthy gradient flow across regions characterized by intense
localized shear and steep stress variations, which are typical of viscoelastic flows.

\subsection{Multi-Layer Perceptron Architecture}
\label{subsec:mlp_architecture}

By arranging artificial neurons into sequential layers, we construct a Multi-Layer
Perceptron (MLP). The network maps an input vector $\boldsymbol{x}$ to an output
prediction $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ by propagating information forward through $L$
successive layers:
\begin{align}
    \boldsymbol{h}^{(1)} &= \sigma\left( \boldsymbol{W}^{(1)} \boldsymbol{x} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(1)} \right) , \nonumber \\
    \boldsymbol{h}^{(l)} &= \sigma\left( \boldsymbol{W}^{(l)} \boldsymbol{h}^{(l-1)} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(l)} \right) , \quad \text{for } l = 2, \dots, L-1 , \label{eq:mlp_layers} \\
    \hat{\boldsymbol{u}} &= \boldsymbol{W}^{(L)} \boldsymbol{h}^{(L-1)} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(L)} , \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol{h}^{(l)}$ denotes the hidden feature vector at layer $l$, and
$\boldsymbol{W}^{(l)}$ and $\boldsymbol{b}^{(l)}$ represent the weight matrix and
bias vector of that layer. In the context of this thesis, the inputs $\boldsymbol{x}
= (x, y)$ are spatial coordinates within the fluid domain, while the outputs
$\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ correspond to the continuous physical fields of interest, such
as the stream function $\psi$, the pressure $p$, and the extra-stress tensor
components $\boldsymbol{\tau}$.

A central theoretical property of MLPs is their universal approximation capability
\cite{cybenko1989approximation, hornik1989multilayer}: a feedforward network with
sufficient width and smooth non-linear activations can approximate any continuous
function on a closed domain to an arbitrary degree of accuracy.

\subsubsection{Weight Initialization: Glorot Scheme and Output Zero-Initialization}
\label{subsubsec:weight_initialization}

The initialization of the network weights and biases strongly influences optimization
stability. To avoid vanishing or exploding signals across deep layers, the hidden
layer weights are initialized using the Xavier/Glorot normal distribution
\cite{goodfellow2016deep}, which scales the variance of the initial parameters
according to the width of adjacent layers.

For the final output layers representing the extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$
and the pressure $p$, we adopt a strategic zero-initialization ($\boldsymbol{W}^{(L)}
= \boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{b}^{(L)} = \boldsymbol{0}$). This choice is motivated
by two practical reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Thermodynamic Baseline}: In fluid mechanics, a fluid at thermodynamic
        rest has zero extra-stress ($\boldsymbol{\tau} = \boldsymbol{0}$).
        Zero-initialization guarantees that at iteration zero, the network outputs
        correspond to the unperturbed resting state.
    \item \textbf{Suppression of Numerical Shocks}: If output weights were randomly
        initialized, initial stress predictions would exhibit high-frequency spatial
        noise across the domain. Evaluating these random fluctuations inside
        non-linear constitutive equations—which contain quadratic stress products and
        convective gradients—would cause initial residual losses to spike violently,
        destabilizing the optimization. Initializing the output layer to zero ensures
        smooth, bounded initial residuals.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Loss Functions and Optimization Algorithms}
\label{subsec:optimization_solvers}

Training a neural network consists of finding the optimal set of parameters
$\boldsymbol{\theta} = \{ \boldsymbol{W}^{(l)}, \boldsymbol{b}^{(l)} \}$ that
minimizes a scalar loss function $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$. The loss
measures how well the network's predictions match desired targets, typically using
the Mean Squared Error (MSE):
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) - \boldsymbol{u}_i^{\mathrm{target}} \right\|_2^2 .
    \label{eq:mse_loss}
\end{equation}

The parameters are updated iteratively in the direction that reduces the loss,
following the principle of gradient descent:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L} ,
    \label{eq:gradient_descent_update}
\end{equation}
where $\eta > 0$ is the learning rate, which controls the step size taken along the
negative gradient.

In this work, training is carried out using a two-stage optimization strategy:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{First-Order Optimization (Adam)}: The Adaptive Moment Estimation
        optimizer \cite{kingma2014adam} dynamically adjusts the learning rate for
        each individual parameter based on running estimates of past gradients. This
        makes Adam robust and effective at exploring complex loss landscapes during
        the early stages of training.
    \item \textbf{Second-Order Quasi-Newton Optimization (L-BFGS)}: Once the solution
        is close to a minimum, the Limited-memory Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno
        (L-BFGS) algorithm uses curvature information to achieve faster, super-linear
        convergence. In scientific problems, L-BFGS is crucial for refining the
        solution and driving physical residuals down to high accuracy ($10^{-5} -
        10^{-7}$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Backpropagation and Computational Graphs}
\label{subsec:backpropagation}

To update the parameters via gradient descent, the network must compute the
derivative of the loss with respect to every weight and bias, denoted by
$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}$. Evaluating these derivatives numerically
by perturbing each parameter one by one would be computationally infeasible.

Instead, neural networks compute exact gradients efficiently using the
\textit{backpropagation} algorithm \cite{goodfellow2016deep}. The execution of a
neural network can be viewed as a directed computational graph, where inputs flow
forward through mathematical operations to produce the predictions and the final loss
value. Backpropagation applies the classical chain rule of calculus in reverse order,
starting from the loss and propagating sensitivities backward through the graph.

Through this reverse-mode evaluation, the gradients with respect to all parameters in
the network are computed simultaneously in a single backward pass, at a computational
cost roughly equivalent to that of a single forward evaluation. This computational
efficiency is what makes training deep models practical.

\section{Why Neural Networks? Capabilities and Physical Limitations}
\label{sec:why_neural_networks}

\subsection{Capabilities and Universal Function Approximation}
\label{subsec:capabilities_universal}
Neural networks offer attractive mathematical and computational properties for
scientific computing:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Universal Approximation Property}: According to the foundational
        theorems of Hornik et al. \cite{hornik1989multilayer} and Cybenko
        \cite{cybenko1989approximation}, a feedforward neural network with at least
        one hidden layer and continuous non-linear activation functions can
        approximate any continuous function on a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^d$ to
        arbitrary accuracy, given sufficient network width. While this theorem
        guarantees the representational capacity of neural networks for continuous
        physical fields, it is non-constructive; it does not ensure that
        unconstrained empirical loss optimization will discover physically consistent
        parameters from sparse data.
    \item \textbf{Meshless Continuous Representation}: Unlike grid-based numerical
        methods where state variables exist exclusively at discrete vertices or cell
        centers, neural networks define continuous, differentiable field mappings
        over the entire spatial domain.
    \item \textbf{Hardware and Tensor Acceleration}: Neural network evaluations rely
        primarily on dense matrix operations that map directly onto massively
        parallel accelerator architectures (GPUs and TPUs), decoupling spatial
        evaluation cost from geometric grid connectivity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Critical Bottlenecks of Purely Data-Driven Machine Learning}
\label{subsec:black_box_limits}
Despite their high expressivity, deploying purely data-driven, black-box neural
networks to fluid dynamics and non-Newtonian mechanics reveals severe limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data Scarcity in Experimental Rheology}: Deep neural networks
        typically require voluminous labeled datasets. In experimental fluid
        mechanics and rheology, acquiring dense volumetric velocity, stress, and
        pressure data via techniques such as Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) or
        laser Doppler anemometry is challenging, noisy, or experimentally
        impossible—particularly for internal stress components.
    \item \textbf{Violation of Physical Conservation Laws}: Purely data-driven networks
        optimize statistical correlations over discrete training sets without
        respecting physical principles. Consequently, their predictions routinely
        violate mass conservation ($\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u} \neq 0$) and linear
        momentum balance, producing unphysical flow features and failing when
        evaluated outside the narrow envelope of training conditions.
    \item \textbf{Overfitting to Sparse and Noisy Measurements}: In low-data regimes,
        over-parameterized models readily fit experimental noise or sparse sensor
        readings with minimal training error, but develop high-frequency,
        non-physical oscillations across unmonitored subdomains.
    \item \textbf{Black-Box Opacity and Lack of Parameter Discoverability}: Standard
        architectures lack physical interpretability and cannot infer underlying
        governing material parameters—such as fluid relaxation times or polymeric
        viscosities—from observed flow kinematics.
\end{itemize}

These fundamental shortcomings motivated the development of Physics-Informed Neural
Networks, which embed mathematical governing equations directly into the loss
function.

\section{The Physics-Informed Neural Network Paradigm}
\label{sec:pinn_paradigm}

The Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) framework, formalized by Raissi,
Perdikaris, and Karniadakis \cite{raissi2019physics}, integrates differential
equations directly into deep learning optimization, penalizing deviations from
physical laws without relying on spatial discretization meshes.

\subsection{Physics-Constrained Loss Formulation}
\label{subsec:pinn_loss_formulation}
Consider a general non-linear differential operator $\mathcal{F}$ governing a
continuous field $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})$ over an open spatial domain $\Omega
\subset \mathbb{R}^d$ bounded by $\partial \Omega$:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{F}[\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}); \boldsymbol{\lambda}] &= \boldsymbol{0} , \quad \boldsymbol{x} \in \Omega , \label{eq:general_pde_operator} \\
    \mathcal{B}[\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})] &= \boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}) , \quad \boldsymbol{x} \in \partial \Omega , \label{eq:general_bc_operator}
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ represents physical parameters (e.g., relaxation time,
viscosity) and $\mathcal{B}$ denotes boundary differential operators.

In a PINN, the unknown solution $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})$ is approximated by
an MLP $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta})$. Substituting
$\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ into the governing equations defines the differential
residual:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \equiv \mathcal{F}[\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}); \boldsymbol{\lambda}] .
    \label{eq:pde_residual_def}
\end{equation}

The network parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ (and optionally the unknown physical
parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$) are trained jointly by minimizing a composite
multi-objective loss function:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = w_{\mathrm{data}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} + w_{\mathrm{bc}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} + w_{\mathrm{pde}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} ,
    \label{eq:composite_loss_pinn}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{data}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{data}}} \left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_i^{\mathrm{data}}) - \boldsymbol{u}_i^{\mathrm{data}} \right\|_2^2 , \label{eq:loss_data_comp} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{bc}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bc}}} \left\| \mathcal{B}[\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}})] - \boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}}) \right\|_2^2 , \label{eq:loss_bc_comp} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{pde}}} \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{pde}}} \left\| \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}}; \boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \right\|_2^2 . \label{eq:loss_pde_comp}
\end{align}
Here, $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_i^{\mathrm{data}} \}$ denotes sparse experimental or
synthetic measurements, $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}} \}$ represents points
sampled along the physical boundaries $\partial \Omega$, and $\{
\boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}} \}$ designates unlabelled interior
\textit{collocation points} scattered across $\Omega$.

\subsection{Deep Dive: Standard Supervised Training vs. PINN Optimization}
\label{subsec:deep_dive_training}
The operational differences between conventional deep learning training and
physics-informed optimization are fundamental:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Loss Surface and Inductive Bias}: In conventional supervised
        training, the network optimizes an empirical risk function over a finite
        discrete set of input-output pairs $\mathcal{D} = \{ (\boldsymbol{x}_i,
        \boldsymbol{y}_i) \}_{i=1}^N$. Between adjacent training points, the network
        is mathematically unconstrained. In over-parameterized regimes, this lack of
        constraint allows the network to develop high-frequency oscillations that fit
        the sample points perfectly while completely failing on the underlying
        continuous function (overfitting).
    
    Conversely, in PINN training, the loss function incorporates the PDE residual
    $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}$ evaluated at tens of thousands of interior
    collocation points $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}} \}$. The differential
    operator $\mathcal{F}$ acts as an exact, infinite-dimensional \textit{inductive
    bias}. A network cannot form arbitrary interpolating curves through sparse data
    points without incurring massive penalties from spatial derivative mismatches
    (e.g., curvature, convective terms, or pressure gradients). Thus, \textbf{PINNs
    do not overfit} because the continuous physical laws constrain the entire
    functional space across the whole domain $\Omega$.

    \item \textbf{The Automatic Differentiation (AD) Analogy}:
    The foundational computational mechanism enabling PINNs is Automatic
    Differentiation (AD) \cite{baydin2018automatic}. In conventional deep learning,
    backpropagation applies AD to evaluate derivatives of the scalar loss with
    respect to network parameters:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Backpropagation (Classical DL):} \quad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}} .
    \end{equation}
    In PINNs, the exact same computational graph and reverse-mode accumulation chain
    are employed to evaluate derivatives with respect to the spatial input
    coordinates:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Coordinate AD (PINNs):} \quad \frac{\partial \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}}, \quad \frac{\partial^2 \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}^2} .
    \end{equation}
    As illustrated in Table~\ref{tab:differentiation_comparison}, coordinate AD
    computes exact analytical derivatives up to machine floating-point precision
    without requiring discrete difference stencils.

    \item \textbf{Meshless Formulation}:
    Traditional CFD solvers (FEM, FVM, FDM) require a spatial grid that discretizes
    the domain into cells or elements, necessitating element connectivity lists, edge
    neighbor tables, and assembled global stiffness matrices. Grid generation for
    complex geometries (such as the four-roll mill with narrow gaps) is
    computationally intensive and prone to element distortion, mesh skewness, and
    numerical diffusion.
    
    In sharp contrast, PINN collocation points $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}}
    \}$ are treated as an unorganized point cloud in $\mathbb{R}^d$. They require no
    topological connectivity, no cell volumes, and no adjacency relationships.
    Collocation points can be redistributed dynamically or concentrated locally in
    steep boundary layers without re-meshing overhead.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Foundational Literature Benchmarks}
\label{sec:literature_benchmarks}

\subsection{Navier--Stokes Flow Past a Cylinder (Raissi et al., 2019)}
\label{subsec:cylinder_benchmark}
In their foundational study, Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis
\cite{raissi2019physics} demonstrated the capabilities of PINNs on the canonical
benchmark of two-dimensional unsteady laminar flow past a circular cylinder at
Reynolds number $Re = 100$, characterized by periodic vortex shedding (the Von Kármán
vortex street).

\subsubsection{Problem Formulation and Inverse Discovery}
The governing non-dimensional incompressible Navier--Stokes equations are formulated as:
\begin{align}
    u_t + \lambda_1 (u u_x + v u_y) &= -p_x + \lambda_2 (u_{xx} + u_{yy}) , \label{eq:cylinder_ns_u} \\
    v_t + \lambda_1 (u v_x + v v_y) &= -p_y + \lambda_2 (v_{xx} + v_{yy}) , \label{eq:cylinder_ns_v} \\
    u_x + v_y &= 0 , \label{eq:cylinder_cont}
\end{align}
where $\lambda_1$ denotes the convective acceleration coefficient
($\lambda_1^{\mathrm{true}} = 1.0$) and $\lambda_2$ corresponds to the kinematic
viscosity ($\lambda_2^{\mathrm{true}} = 1/Re = 0.01$).

To satisfy the incompressibility condition~\eqref{eq:cylinder_cont} identically,
Raissi et al. adopted the scalar stream function formulation $\psi(t, x, y)$
established in Chapter~\ref{chap:fluidodynamic_background}
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:stream_function_def}):
\begin{equation}
    u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}, \qquad v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} .
    \label{eq:stream_function_cylinder}
\end{equation}
A single neural network with 9 fully-connected hidden layers and 20 neurons per layer
was employed to predict the latent scalar fields $(\psi, p)$ from coordinates $(t, x,
y)$, while simultaneously estimating the unknown physical coefficients $(\lambda_1,
\lambda_2)$. In this classic setup, neurons were activated using the hyperbolic
tangent function:
\begin{equation}
    \tanh(z) = \frac{e^z - e^{-z}}{e^z + e^{-z}} ,
    \label{eq:tanh_activation}
\end{equation}
which is smooth and symmetric around zero, providing continuous spatial derivatives.

\subsubsection{Latent Field Discovery and Parameter Identification}
The network was trained on an ultra-sparse dataset of $N = 5{,}000$ spatio-temporal
velocity points (corresponding to approximately $1\%$ of the computational space-time
volume) using 200{,}000 Adam iterations followed by 50{,}000 L-BFGS steps. This
benchmark illustrated two practical capabilities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latent Pressure Field Discovery}: No pressure measurements were
        provided during training ($N_{\mathrm{data}, p} = 0$). By enforcing the
        momentum residuals~\eqref{eq:cylinder_ns_u}--\eqref{eq:cylinder_ns_v}, the
        network reconstructed the complete spatio-temporal pressure field $p(t, x,
        y)$ solely from velocity data and governing physical laws, recovering the
        reference CFD pressure distribution up to an arbitrary hydrostatic reference
        constant.
    \item \textbf{Parameter Identification Under Noise}: The physical coefficients were
        identified with high accuracy. For clean velocity data, the relative
        estimation errors were $0.08\%$ for $\lambda_1$ and $4.67\%$ for $\lambda_2$.
        When $1\%$ uncorrelated Gaussian noise was added to the velocity data, the
        errors remained stable at $0.17\%$ for $\lambda_1$ and $5.70\%$ for
        $\lambda_2$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The ViscoelasticNet Framework (Thakur et al., 2024)}
\label{subsec:viscoelasticnet_benchmark}
Extending PINNs from Newtonian flows to complex non-Newtonian media, Thakur, Raissi,
and Ardekani \cite{thakur2024viscoelasticnet} introduced \textit{ViscoelasticNet},
published in the \textit{Journal of Non-Newtonian Fluid Mechanics}.

\subsubsection{The Rheological Bottleneck}
Viscoelastic fluids exhibit memory effects, normal stress differences, and
shear-dependent viscosities. Investigating these systems numerically and
experimentally presents distinct challenges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Experimental Limits}: While optical diagnostics (e.g., PIV) capture
        the velocity field $\boldsymbol{u}$, non-intrusive measurements of the
        internal extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ are generally inaccessible
        in arbitrary flow geometries.
    \item \textbf{Numerical Instability (HWNP)}: Traditional grid-based methods (FEM,
        FVM) suffer from loss of convergence at high Weissenberg numbers ($Wi > 0.5$)
        due to hyperbolic stress advection and steep stress gradients near stagnation
        zones, often requiring numerical diffusion (e.g., SUPG) to maintain
        stability.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{The Virtual Rheometer Concept}
ViscoelasticNet addresses these challenges by formulating a physics-informed "virtual
rheometer":
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latent Stress Inference from Velocity}: Using solely sparse velocity
        observations $\boldsymbol{u}$, the framework infers the hidden extra-stress
        tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ and pressure field $p$ throughout the flow domain.
    \item \textbf{Constitutive Model Selection and Parameter Discovery}: By formulating
        a unified constitutive equation encompassing Oldroyd-B, linear PTT, and
        Giesekus models:
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{\tau} + \lambda \overset{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{\tau}} + \frac{\alpha \lambda}{\mu_p} (\boldsymbol{\tau} \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau}) + \frac{\varepsilon \lambda}{\mu_p} \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau}) \boldsymbol{\tau} = 2\mu_p \boldsymbol{D} ,
        \label{eq:unified_constitutive_thakur}
    \end{equation}
    the network simultaneously identifies the relaxation time $\lambda$, polymeric
    viscosity $\mu_p$, and non-linear mobility parameters ($\alpha, \varepsilon$),
    characterizing the constitutive behavior of the fluid.
    \item \textbf{Decoupled Multi-Network Architecture}: ViscoelasticNet deploys three
        distinct sub-networks (Velocity, Stress, and Pressure Nets). Optimization is
        structured into two sequential stages:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Stage 1 (Stress and Rheology)}: Velocity and stress networks are
            optimized jointly by minimizing velocity data mismatch and constitutive
            residuals, identifying rheological parameters $(\lambda, \mu_p, \alpha,
            \varepsilon)$.
        \item \textit{Stage 2 (Pressure and Viscosity)}: The stress network is frozen,
            and the pressure network is optimized alongside solvent viscosity $\mu_s$
            by minimizing momentum residuals.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Validation Benchmarks}: On 2D planar channels (sinusoidal stenosis
        and cross-slot geometries), ViscoelasticNet achieved relative stress errors
        below $5\%$ and demonstrated resilience to noise levels up to $10\%$ on
        velocity observations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Gap and Dissertation Objectives}
\label{sec:literature_gap_objectives}

The formulation of physics-informed neural networks for non-Newtonian flows addresses
two distinct levels of technological and scientific gaps: the fundamental limitations
of classical numerical grid-based solvers, and the specific domain constraints that
remain unaddressed in existing viscoelastic PINN frameworks.

\subsection{Why PINNs for Viscoelastic Flows in Complex Geometries}
\label{subsec:why_pinns_complex_rheology}
In complex polymeric flows, traditional grid-based computational fluid dynamics
methods (Finite Element and Finite Volume Methods) encounter profound numerical
bottlenecks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The High Weissenberg Number Problem (HWNP)}: At elevated elasticity,
        the hyperbolic constitutive advection of macromolecular stress creates
        exponential boundary layers and steep stress gradients near wall boundaries
        and stagnation points. Standard discretization schemes suffer from severe
        loss of convergence and numerical breakdown.
    \item \textbf{Mesh Distortion and Artificial Diffusion}: While numerical
        stabilization methods (such as SUPG and the log-conformation transformation)
        mitigate numerical breakdown, they introduce artificial numerical diffusion
        and demand intensive, geometrically conforming mesh refinement around
        rotating boundaries and narrow gaps.
    \item \textbf{Intractable Inverse Formulation}: Conventional CFD codes are designed
        strictly as forward solvers; extracting unknown rheological properties or
        reconstructing latent stress distributions from experimental velocity
        measurements requires costly iterative outer-loop optimization.
\end{enumerate}
By reformulating flow simulation as a continuous, mesh-free optimization problem
governed by Automatic Differentiation, PINNs bypass element distortion, remain robust
to geometric constraints, and naturally unify forward state prediction with inverse
parameter identification.

\subsection{Advancing Beyond ViscoelasticNet: Specific Dissertation Contributions}
\label{subsec:gap_vs_viscoelasticnet}
While the ViscoelasticNet framework of Thakur et al. \cite{thakur2024viscoelasticnet}
demonstrated the feasibility of PINNs for viscoelastic stress discovery, its
application was confined to open channel domains (stenosis and cross-slot geometries)
under restricted conditions. This dissertation advances the state of the art through
three primary methodological contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Enclosed Domain with Mixed Kinematics (Four-Roll Mill)}: Unlike open
        channel flows governed by net pressure drops, the four-roll mill constitutes
        an enclosed recirculating cavity with zero net flux, driven by rotating
        cylinder boundaries. This geometry features a pure extensional hyperbolic
        stagnation point at the cavity center coupled with intense localized shear
        layers adjacent to the roller surfaces.
    \item \textbf{Exact Mass Conservation via Stream Function}: ViscoelasticNet
        utilized primitive velocity variables $(u, v)$ and enforced incompressibility
        as a soft penalty loss term ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{div}}$). In enclosed
        recirculating flows, soft divergence constraints allow gradual mass leakage.
        Here, we adopt the scalar stream function formulation $\psi$
        (Eq.~\eqref{eq:stream_function_def}), guaranteeing $\nabla \cdot
        \boldsymbol{u} \equiv 0$ analytically and eliminating the continuity residual
        entirely.
    \item \textbf{Substantially Extended Rheological Bounds}: Existing viscoelastic
        PINN studies examined restricted parameter spaces ($\lambda \le
        0.15\,\mathrm{s}$, $\mu_p \le 0.025\,\mathrm{Pa\cdot s}$, $\alpha \le 0.20$).
        In this work, the rheological domain is extended up to an order of magnitude
        higher ($\lambda$ up to $1.0\,\mathrm{s}$, $\mu_p$ up to
        $0.98\,\mathrm{Pa\cdot s}$, and mobility $\alpha$ up to $0.50$), evaluating
        model robustness across pronounced non-linear regimes.
\end{enumerate}

The focus on steady-state creeping flows with physics-informed networks aligns
closely with the most recent developments in computational rheology, notably the work
of Matos, Fernandes, and Alves \cite{matos2026simulation}, corroborating that
physics-informed formulations represent an active and timely frontier for
non-Newtonian flow modeling.

Building upon these foundations, this dissertation develops a staged, multi-head
physics-informed neural network framework tailored to the four-roll mill geometry,
resolving both direct hydrodynamic fields and inverse constitutive parameter
discovery across linear and non-linear viscoelastic regimes.
