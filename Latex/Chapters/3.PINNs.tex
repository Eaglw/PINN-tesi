\chapter{Physics-Informed Neural Networks in Complex Rheology}
\label{chap:pinn_literature}

Feedforward deep neural networks, commonly known as Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs),
provide flexible continuous approximations for non-linear functions \cite{goodfellow2016deep}.
An MLP is formed by composing a sequence of parametric functions, termed \textit{layers}.
Each layer maps its inputs through a linear transformation followed by an element-wise non-linear operation.
By cascading these layers in series, the network can represent continuous functional mappings
of arbitrary complexity without requiring hand-crafted basis functions.

In scientific computing, neural networks offer an appealing feature:
they represent fields as continuous, globally defined functions over spatial coordinates.
Rather than storing state variables at discrete grid vertices,
the network parameterizes physical fields everywhere in the continuum.
However, using purely data-driven neural networks for fluid mechanics presents fundamental challenges:
they require large amounts of labeled data, can violate conservation laws,
and cannot infer latent material properties on their own.

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) address these challenges
by embedding governing partial differential equations directly into the training objective.
In this chapter, we establish the mathematical foundations of neural networks and their optimization,
discuss why continuous neural surrogates are suitable for transport phenomena,
formulate the PINN paradigm, and review foundational benchmarks in complex fluid mechanics.

% 3.1 FUNDAMENTALS OF DEEP LEARNING

\section{Fundamentals of Deep Learning}
\label{sec:dl_fundamentals}

Deep learning models approximate continuous mathematical mappings
by learning hierarchical representations directly from data \cite{goodfellow2016deep}.
In this section, we review the fundamental building blocks of feedforward networks:
the artificial neuron, activation functions, multi-layer architectures,
loss functions, optimization algorithms, and gradient computation.

\subsection{The Artificial Neuron and Non-Linear Activation Functions}
\label{subsec:perceptron_activations}

The elementary computational unit of a neural network is the artificial neuron.
Given an input vector $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$,
a neuron computes an affine combination of the inputs followed by a non-linear activation:
\begin{equation}
    z = \boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x} + b , \qquad a = \sigma(z) ,
    \label{eq:single_neuron}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{w}$ is the weight vector, $b$ is a scalar bias,
and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the activation function.
Non-linear activations are essential:
without them, composing multiple linear layers collapses algebraically into a single linear mapping,
depriving deep architectures of their expressive power.

In classical machine learning, piecewise-linear functions such as the Rectified Linear Unit,
$\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$, are widely favored for their computational efficiency.
However, in scientific computing and fluid mechanics,
the network outputs parameterize continuous physical fields governed by differential equations.
These governing balance laws involve spatial derivatives of first, second, or third order,
such as viscous diffusion $\mu_s \nabla^2 \boldsymbol{u}$ or stress divergence $\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau}$.

Because the first derivative of ReLU is discontinuous and its second derivative is zero almost everywhere,
evaluating a ReLU network inside differential residuals eliminates diffusive and curvature terms.
Physics-informed models therefore require smooth, differentiable activation functions ($\mathcal{C}^2$ or higher).
In this work, we employ the Sigmoid Linear Unit (SiLU, also known as Swish) \cite{goodfellow2016deep}:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SiLU}(z) = \frac{z}{1 + e^{-z}} .
    \label{eq:silu_activation}
\end{equation}
The SiLU activation is smooth, non-monotonic, and self-gated.
Unlike the hyperbolic tangent ($\tanh$), which saturates toward constant values for large positive inputs,
SiLU remains active in the positive regime.
This property maintains healthy gradient propagation in flow regions with intense shear
and steep localized stress gradients, which are characteristic of viscoelastic flows.

\subsection{Multi-Layer Perceptron Architecture}
\label{subsec:mlp_architecture}

By arranging artificial neurons into sequential layers, we construct a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP).
The network maps an input vector $\boldsymbol{x}$ to an output prediction $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$
by propagating information through $L$ successive layers:
\begin{align}
    \boldsymbol{h}^{(1)} &= \sigma\left( \boldsymbol{W}^{(1)} \boldsymbol{x} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(1)} \right) , \nonumber \\
    \boldsymbol{h}^{(l)} &= \sigma\left( \boldsymbol{W}^{(l)} \boldsymbol{h}^{(l-1)} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(l)} \right) , \quad \text{for } l = 2, \dots, L-1 , \label{eq:mlp_layers} \\
    \hat{\boldsymbol{u}} &= \boldsymbol{W}^{(L)} \boldsymbol{h}^{(L-1)} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(L)} , \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol{h}^{(l)}$ denotes the hidden feature vector at layer $l$,
and $\boldsymbol{W}^{(l)}$ and $\boldsymbol{b}^{(l)}$ represent the weight matrix and bias vector of that layer.
In our implementation, the inputs $\boldsymbol{x} = (x, y)$ are spatial coordinates within the fluid cavity,
while the outputs $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ parameterize the physical state variables:
the stream function $\psi$, hydrodynamic pressure $p$, and extra-stress tensor components $\boldsymbol{\tau}$.

A central theoretical property of MLPs is universal function approximation
\cite{cybenko1989approximation, hornik1989multilayer}:
a feedforward network with sufficient width and smooth non-linear activations
can approximate any continuous function on a compact domain to arbitrary precision.
However, universal approximation guarantees only the existence of a suitable set of weights,
not that standard gradient-based optimization will automatically find them
or that spatial derivatives will match the physical solution with equal fidelity.

\subsubsection{Weight Initialization: Glorot Scheme and Output Zero-Initialization}
\label{subsubsec:weight_initialization}

The initialization of network weights strongly influences optimization dynamics.
To prevent vanishing or exploding activations across deep layers,
hidden layer weights are initialized using the Xavier/Glorot normal distribution \cite{goodfellow2016deep},
which scales parameter variances inversely with layer widths.

For the final linear layers predicting the extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ and pressure $p$,
we initialize weights and biases to zero ($\boldsymbol{W}^{(L)} = \boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{b}^{(L)} = \boldsymbol{0}$).
This choice provides two practical benefits:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Physical state of rest}:
    In fluid mechanics, a fluid at rest carries zero extra-stress ($\boldsymbol{\tau} = \boldsymbol{0}$).
    Zero-initialization ensures that at iteration zero,
    the stress network starts from an unperturbed resting state.
    \item \textbf{Suppression of initial gradient shocks}:
    If output weights were initialized randomly,
    initial stress predictions would contain high-frequency spatial fluctuations.
    Evaluating these fluctuations inside non-linear constitutive equations,
    which involve quadratic stress products and convective gradients,
    would generate large initial residual spikes that can destabilize optimization.
    Zero-initializing the output layer ensures bounded, well-behaved initial residuals.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Loss Functions and Optimization Algorithms}
\label{subsec:optimization_solvers}

Training an MLP consists of finding parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} = \{ \boldsymbol{W}^{(l)}, \boldsymbol{b}^{(l)} \}$
that minimize a scalar objective function $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$.
In supervised regression, the discrepancy between predictions and reference values
is commonly evaluated using the Mean Squared Error (MSE):
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) - \boldsymbol{u}_i^{\mathrm{target}} \right\|_2^2 .
    \label{eq:mse_loss}
\end{equation}

Parameters are updated iteratively along the negative gradient:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L} ,
    \label{eq:gradient_descent_update}
\end{equation}
where $\eta > 0$ is the learning rate governing the step size.

In scientific PINN applications, optimization commonly employs a two-tier strategy:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{First-order adaptive optimization (Adam)}:
    The Adaptive Moment Estimation algorithm \cite{kingma2014adam} scales parameter updates
    using running estimates of past gradient moments.
    This enables Adam to traverse non-convex loss landscapes effectively during early exploration.
    \item \textbf{Second-order quasi-Newton optimization (L-BFGS)}:
    Once the parameters reach the basin of attraction of a minimum,
    the Limited-memory Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno (L-BFGS) algorithm
    incorporates curvature approximations to achieve rapid, deterministic convergence,
    driving PDE residuals to low tolerance levels.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Backpropagation and Computational Graphs}
\label{subsec:backpropagation}

To update network parameters via gradient descent,
we compute the partial derivatives of the scalar loss with respect to all weights and biases, $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}$.
Evaluating these gradients through individual numerical perturbations would be computationally prohibitive.

Instead, neural networks compute analytical gradients using the \textit{backpropagation} algorithm \cite{goodfellow2016deep}.
The forward evaluation is structured as a directed acyclic graph of mathematical operations.
Backpropagation applies the calculus chain rule in reverse order,
starting from the loss scalar and propagating sensitivities backward through the graph.
This reverse-mode automatic differentiation evaluates all parameter gradients simultaneously
at a computational cost comparable to a single forward pass,
making deep network training feasible.

\section{Why Neural Networks? Capabilities and Physical Limitations}
\label{sec:why_neural_networks}

\subsection{Capabilities and Universal Function Approximation}
\label{subsec:capabilities_universal}

Neural networks offer distinctive characteristics that make them attractive for continuous physical modeling:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Continuous and Meshless Representation}: Traditional grid-based methods 
    (Finite Element, Finite Volume, and Finite Difference Methods) represent state variables 
    exclusively at discrete vertices or cell centers, relying on explicit mesh connectivity. 
    In contrast, a neural network provides a continuous, globally defined mapping $\boldsymbol{x} \mapsto \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x})$ 
    over the entire domain, capable of being evaluated at arbitrary spatial coordinates. 
    However, being meshless does not imply freedom from geometric considerations: defining the domain, 
    enforcing boundary conditions, and designing effective collocation point distributions remain essential 
    and influential steps.
    \item \textbf{High-Dimensional Functional Approximation}: The universal approximation capacity 
    allows neural networks to represent complex, coupled physical fields (velocities, pressures, and stress tensors) 
    within a unified functional representation, without requiring specialized interpolation elements for each variable.
    \item \textbf{Hardware Acceleration and Differentiability}: Modern deep learning frameworks 
    are built upon dense linear algebra operations optimized for parallel accelerator hardware (GPUs and TPUs), 
    and provide built-in automatic differentiation for computing spatial derivatives of the represented fields.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Critical Bottlenecks of Purely Data-Driven Machine Learning}
\label{subsec:black_box_limits}

Despite their high expressivity, deploying purely data-driven black-box networks
to fluid mechanics and non-Newtonian rheology reveals fundamental limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data scarcity in experimental rheology}:
    Deep neural networks typically rely on large labeled datasets.
    In fluid experiments, acquiring dense velocity fields via Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) is labor-intensive,
    while non-intrusive measurements of internal extra-stress components are generally impossible in complex geometries.
    \item \textbf{Violation of fundamental conservation laws}:
    Purely data-driven models minimize empirical loss on discrete observations without physical constraints.
    Consequently, their predictions routinely violate incompressibility ($\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u} \neq 0$),
    momentum balance, and constitutive laws, producing non-physical artifacts when interpolating or extrapolating.
    \item \textbf{Overfitting and spurious oscillations}:
    In sparse-data regimes, over-parameterized networks can easily fit measurement noise,
    yielding low training errors while developing spurious high-frequency oscillations in unmonitored regions.
    \item \textbf{Inability to discover governing parameters}:
    Black-box models learn input-output mappings,
    but cannot extract underlying physical properties---such as relaxation times or polymeric viscosities---that govern flow dynamics.
\end{itemize}

These limitations show that data alone are insufficient for scientific modeling in rheology,
motivating frameworks that embed physical conservation laws directly into the loss function.

\section{The Physics-Informed Neural Network Paradigm}
\label{sec:pinn_paradigm}

The Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) framework, formalized by Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis \cite{raissi2019physics},
addresses the shortcomings of purely data-driven modeling
by incorporating differential governing equations directly into the network training objective.

\subsection{Physics-Constrained Loss Formulation}
\label{subsec:pinn_loss_formulation}

Consider a general non-linear differential operator $\mathcal{F}$ governing a physical state $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})$
over a spatial domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ bounded by $\partial \Omega$:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{F}[\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}); \boldsymbol{\lambda}] &= \boldsymbol{0} , \quad \boldsymbol{x} \in \Omega , \label{eq:general_pde_operator} \\
    \mathcal{B}[\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})] &= \boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}) , \quad \boldsymbol{x} \in \partial \Omega , \label{eq:general_bc_operator}
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ represents physical parameters (such as viscosities or relaxation times)
and $\mathcal{B}$ denotes boundary operators.

In a PINN, the unknown physical fields $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})$ are parameterized
by a neural network $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta})$.
Evaluating the governing equations on the network outputs defines the differential residual:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \equiv \mathcal{F}[\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}); \boldsymbol{\lambda}] .
    \label{eq:pde_residual_def}
\end{equation}
The network weights $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, alongside unknown physical parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ in inverse problems,
are optimized by minimizing a composite multi-objective loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = w_{\mathrm{data}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} + w_{\mathrm{bc}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} + w_{\mathrm{pde}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} ,
    \label{eq:composite_loss_pinn}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{data}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{data}}} \left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_i^{\mathrm{data}}) - \boldsymbol{u}_i^{\mathrm{data}} \right\|_2^2 , \label{eq:loss_data_comp} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{bc}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bc}}} \left\| \mathcal{B}[\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}})] - \boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}}) \right\|_2^2 , \label{eq:loss_bc_comp} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{pde}}} \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{pde}}} \left\| \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}}; \boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \right\|_2^2 . \label{eq:loss_pde_comp}
\end{align}
Here, $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_i^{\mathrm{data}} \}$ denotes available empirical measurements,
$\{ \boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}} \}$ represents points sampled along physical boundaries $\partial \Omega$,
and $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}} \}$ designates interior \textit{collocation points} scattered across $\Omega$.

This formulation naturally supports two complementary problem settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Forward problems}:
    The physical parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ are known.
    The network optimizes its weights $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ to solve for the continuous fields $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x})$,
    acting as a meshless continuous PDE solver.
    \item \textbf{Inverse problems}:
    One or more constitutive parameters in $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ are unknown.
    The network simultaneously reconstructs the unobserved latent fields
    and identifies the unknown parameters by combining empirical velocity observations $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}$
    with physical consistency constraints $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}$.
\end{itemize}

A key methodological principle of this thesis is the distinction between
field reconstruction accuracy and parameter identifiability.
A network may fit observed velocity fields accurately,
yet inferred rheological parameters may remain uncertain if the kinematics lack sufficient variation
or if the loss landscape exhibits degeneracies.
Embedding physical equations provides essential structure,
but does not automatically guarantee unique parameter recovery.

\subsection{Standard Supervised Training vs. PINN Optimization}
\label{subsec:deep_dive_training}

Several operational features distinguish physics-informed training from conventional supervised learning:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Training as a boundary-value solver}:
    In classical machine learning, a model is trained on a sample set to generalize to unseen statistical distributions.
    In PINNs, \textit{training is the numerical solver}.
    The network is not designed to predict flows across arbitrary unseen geometries;
    rather, optimization directly solves the boundary-value problem on the specified domain $\Omega$.
    Available measurements $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}$ act as an active data-assimilation anchor
    guiding the network toward the true physical state.

    \item \textbf{Physical regularization and inductive bias}:
    In standard supervised training, the network is unconstrained between data points.
    In PINNs, evaluating differential residuals $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}$ across interior collocation points
    penalizes non-physical gradients and curvatures.
    This analytical inductive bias restricts the admissible solution space.
    However, proper weighting between data and PDE terms remains essential:
    poorly tuned weights can still bias the optimization or slow down convergence.

    \item \textbf{Coordinate Automatic Differentiation (AD)}:
    Classical deep learning uses backpropagation to differentiate the loss with respect to network parameters:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Backpropagation (Classical DL):} \quad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}} .
    \end{equation}
    In PINNs, the same reverse-mode automatic differentiation engine computes spatial derivatives
    of the predicted state fields with respect to input spatial coordinates:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Coordinate AD (PINNs):} \quad \frac{\partial \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}}, \quad \frac{\partial^2 \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}^2} .
    \end{equation}
    As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:differentiation_comparison},
    coordinate AD evaluates exact derivatives of the neural functional representation along the computational graph,
    avoiding the spatial truncation error $\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2)$ of finite-difference stencils.
    The accuracy of these derivatives with respect to the true physical field
    depends on how closely the network itself approximates the physical solution.

    \item \textbf{Collocation point sampling}:
    Unlike traditional CFD solvers that require conforming meshes and element connectivity tables,
    PINNs evaluate differential residuals at scattered collocation points $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}} \}$.
    This meshless feature simplifies domain representation,
    though adequate point density remains necessary in boundary layers and near stagnation regions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Foundational Literature Benchmarks}
\label{sec:literature_benchmarks}

\subsection{Navier--Stokes Flow Past a Cylinder (Raissi et al., 2019)}
\label{subsec:cylinder_benchmark}

In their foundational paper, Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis \cite{raissi2019physics}
demonstrated the capability of PINNs on laminar vortex shedding past a circular cylinder at $Re = 100$.

\subsubsection{Problem Formulation and Inverse Discovery}
The non-dimensional incompressible Navier--Stokes equations were formulated as:
\begin{align}
    u_t + \lambda_1 (u u_x + v u_y) &= -p_x + \lambda_2 (u_{xx} + u_{yy}) , \label{eq:cylinder_ns_u} \\
    v_t + \lambda_1 (u v_x + v v_y) &= -p_y + \lambda_2 (v_{xx} + v_{yy}) , \label{eq:cylinder_ns_v} \\
    u_x + v_y &= 0 , \label{eq:cylinder_cont}
\end{align}
where $\lambda_1 = 1.0$ governs convective acceleration
and $\lambda_2 = 1/Re = 0.01$ represents kinematic viscosity.

To satisfy incompressibility identically, the authors parameterized velocities
via a stream function $\psi(t, x, y)$ ($u = \partial\psi/\partial y, v = -\partial\psi/\partial x$).
A neural network with 9 hidden layers and 20 neurons per layer, activated by $\tanh$,
predicted $(\psi, p)$ while treating $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ as learnable parameters.

\subsubsection{Latent Field Discovery and Parameter Identification}
The network was trained on $N = 5{,}000$ spatio-temporal velocity observations in the cylinder wake.
The benchmark demonstrated two key capabilities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latent pressure recovery}:
    Without any pressure measurements, the network reconstructed the pressure field $p(t, x, y)$
    solely by enforcing the momentum balance, matching CFD reference data up to an arbitrary constant.
    \item \textbf{Parameter estimation under noise}:
    On clean data, parameter errors were $0.08\%$ for $\lambda_1$ and $4.67\%$ for $\lambda_2$.
    Under $1\%$ uncorrelated Gaussian noise, errors remained controlled at $0.17\%$ and $5.70\%$.
\end{itemize}
While this benchmark validated simultaneous field reconstruction and parameter identification,
it was restricted to linear Newtonian fluids in an open domain.

\subsection{The ViscoelasticNet Framework (Thakur et al., 2024)}
\label{subsec:viscoelasticnet_benchmark}

Extending physics-informed methods to non-Newtonian media,
Thakur, Raissi, and Ardekani \cite{thakur2024viscoelasticnet} introduced \textit{ViscoelasticNet},
applying PINNs to polymeric flows governed by differential constitutive equations.

\subsubsection{The Rheological Bottleneck}
Viscoelastic fluid simulation presents two major challenges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Experimental limitations}:
    Non-intrusive measurements of internal extra-stresses $\boldsymbol{\tau}$
    are generally unavailable in complex geometries.
    \item \textbf{Numerical instability (HWNP)}:
    In standard grid-based solvers (FEM, FVM),
    elevated elasticity triggers the High-Weissenberg Number Problem (HWNP),
    where steep stress boundary layers cause convergence breakdown unless stabilized numerically.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{The Virtual Rheometer Concept}
To address these bottlenecks, ViscoelasticNet proposed an inverse framework operating as a "virtual rheometer":
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latent stress inference}:
    Given velocity measurements $\boldsymbol{u}$, the network infers unobserved extra-stresses $\boldsymbol{\tau}$
    and pressure $p$ by enforcing constitutive and momentum residuals.
    \item \textbf{Unified constitutive formulation}:
    The authors formulated a unified constitutive expression encompassing Oldroyd-B, linear PTT, and Giesekus models:
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{\tau} + \lambda \overset{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{\tau}} + \frac{\alpha \lambda}{\mu_p} (\boldsymbol{\tau} \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau}) + \frac{\varepsilon \lambda}{\mu_p} \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau}) \boldsymbol{\tau} = 2\mu_p \boldsymbol{D} ,
        \label{eq:unified_constitutive_thakur}
    \end{equation}
    seeking to identify $\lambda, \mu_p, \alpha$, and $\varepsilon$.
    \item \textbf{Decoupled two-stage training}:
    Optimization was separated into two sequential stages:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Stage 1 (Stress and Rheology)}: Velocity and stress networks were optimized jointly
        using velocity data and constitutive residuals, learning rheological parameters $(\lambda, \mu_p, \alpha, \varepsilon)$.
        \item \textit{Stage 2 (Pressure and Viscosity)}: The stress network was frozen,
        and the pressure network was trained alongside solvent viscosity $\mu_s$ using momentum residuals.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Investigated geometries}:
    ViscoelasticNet was evaluated on two open geometries: a planar stenosed channel and a cross-slot flow.
    The authors reported relative stress errors below $5\%$ and demonstrated stability under synthetic velocity noise up to $10\%$.
\end{itemize}

These results confirmed that PINNs can infer non-Newtonian stresses from velocity fields in open channel flows.
However, their formulation focused on domains with net through-flow and convective pressure drops.
In the next chapter, we address the open problem of enclosed recirculating flows:
we formulate a stream-function PINN for the four-roll mill geometry,
evaluate forward field accuracy, and investigate the boundaries of inverse parameter discovery.
