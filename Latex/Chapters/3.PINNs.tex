\chapter{Physics-Informed Neural Networks in Complex Rheology}
\label{chap:pinn_literature}

Feedforward deep networks, commonly known as Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs),
represent the quintessential architecture of deep learning \cite{goodfellow2016deep}.
Conceptually, an MLP is a parametric function defined by composing together a
sequence of simpler parametric functions. Each of these constituent functions has
multiple inputs and outputs, and in neural network terminology is called a
\textit{layer}. The scalar outputs computed within each layer are referred to as
\textit{units} or \textit{features}. Although each unit performs a relatively simple
mathematical mapping of its inputs, chaining them in succession allows the network as
a whole to express continuous functions of arbitrary complexity.

The structural dimensions of a network are conventionally characterized by its
\textit{width}—the number of units in a given layer—and its \textit{depth}—the total
number of layers. Having significant width and depth provides deep architectures with
remarkable representational power, making them the foundational technology behind
modern machine learning applications.

To appreciate the paradigm shift introduced by deep neural networks, it is useful to
contrast them with traditional machine learning algorithms, such as linear
regression, logistic classifiers, and kernel methods. Classical algorithms operate by
applying a linear transformation to a fixed set of features. These methods can indeed
learn non-linear behaviors, but their non-linearity is rigid: it is non-linear only
in the original input space, while remaining strictly linear in a feature space that
must be pre-defined and engineered manually by human experts. Neural networks
fundamentally overcome this limitation by automating the design of features. Rather
than relying on manual feature engineering, an MLP learns the optimal representations
directly from data.

In this architecture, the layers between the inputs and the final outputs are termed
\textit{hidden layers}. They are called hidden because their internal values are not
specified or observed in the training data; instead, the optimization algorithm must
autonomously determine how to organize these intermediate representations to solve
the target problem. By transforming inputs through successive hidden layers, neural
networks can approximate complex continuous relationships.

In this chapter, we explore how these feedforward architectures form the
computational core of scientific machine learning. We begin by reviewing the
fundamentals of neural networks and their optimization. We then examine why
continuous neural representations are compelling for physical modeling, while
discussing the critical limitations of purely data-driven black-box approaches. This
analysis naturally motivates the Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) paradigm,
followed by a review of foundational literature benchmarks and their applications
in complex non-Newtonian flows.

\section{Fundamentals of Deep Learning}
\label{sec:dl_fundamentals}

Deep learning algorithms approximate continuous mathematical functions through
multi-layer architectures that learn internal representations from data
\cite{goodfellow2016deep}. In this section, we present the essential building blocks
of these networks, focusing on their mathematical structure, smooth activation
functions, optimization algorithms, and gradient evaluation.

\subsection{The Artificial Neuron and Non-Linear Activation Functions}
\label{subsec:perceptron_activations}

The elementary processing unit of a neural network is the artificial neuron. Given an
input vector $\boldsymbol{x}$, a neuron computes an affine combination of the inputs
followed by a scalar non-linear mapping:
\begin{equation}
    z = \boldsymbol{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{x} + b , \qquad a = \sigma(z) ,
    \label{eq:single_neuron}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{w}$ is the weight vector, $b$ is a scalar bias, and
$\sigma(\cdot)$ is the activation function. The activation function introduces
non-linearity into the model. Without non-linear activations, composing multiple
linear layers would simply collapse mathematically into another single linear
mapping, eliminating the expressive advantage of deep architectures.

In standard deep learning applications such as image recognition, piecewise-linear
activations—most notably the Rectified Linear Unit ($\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0,
z)$)—are widely adopted due to their computational simplicity. However, in scientific
computing and fluid mechanics, the network's outputs represent continuous physical
fields governed by partial differential equations (PDEs), such as velocities,
pressures, and stresses. These governing laws involve spatial derivatives of first,
second, or higher order (such as viscous laplacians $\nabla^2 \boldsymbol{u}$ or
stress divergences $\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau}$).

Because the first derivative of ReLU is discontinuous and its second derivative is
zero almost everywhere ($\text{ReLU}''(z) \equiv 0$), evaluating a ReLU network
inside differential equations would yield zero for diffusive and curvature terms. For
this reason, physics-informed models strictly require smooth, infinitely
differentiable activation functions ($\mathcal{C}^\infty$). In this work, we employ
the Sigmoid Linear Unit (SiLU, also known as Swish) \cite{goodfellow2016deep}:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SiLU}(z) = \frac{z}{1 + e^{-z}} .
    \label{eq:silu_activation}
\end{equation}
The SiLU activation is smooth, non-monotonic, and self-gated. Unlike classical
functions such as the hyperbolic tangent ($\tanh$) that saturate toward constant
limits for large inputs, SiLU remains active in the positive regime. This property
helps maintain healthy gradient flow across regions characterized by intense
localized shear and steep stress variations, which are typical of viscoelastic flows.

\subsection{Multi-Layer Perceptron Architecture}
\label{subsec:mlp_architecture}

By arranging artificial neurons into sequential layers, we construct a Multi-Layer
Perceptron (MLP). The network maps an input vector $\boldsymbol{x}$ to an output
prediction $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ by propagating information forward through $L$
successive layers:
\begin{align}
    \boldsymbol{h}^{(1)} &= \sigma\left( \boldsymbol{W}^{(1)} \boldsymbol{x} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(1)} \right) , \nonumber \\
    \boldsymbol{h}^{(l)} &= \sigma\left( \boldsymbol{W}^{(l)} \boldsymbol{h}^{(l-1)} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(l)} \right) , \quad \text{for } l = 2, \dots, L-1 , \label{eq:mlp_layers} \\
    \hat{\boldsymbol{u}} &= \boldsymbol{W}^{(L)} \boldsymbol{h}^{(L-1)} + \boldsymbol{b}^{(L)} , \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol{h}^{(l)}$ denotes the hidden feature vector at layer $l$, and
$\boldsymbol{W}^{(l)}$ and $\boldsymbol{b}^{(l)}$ represent the weight matrix and
bias vector of that layer. In the context of this thesis, the inputs $\boldsymbol{x}
= (x, y)$ are spatial coordinates within the fluid domain, while the outputs
$\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ correspond to the continuous physical fields of interest, such
as the stream function $\psi$, the pressure $p$, and the extra-stress tensor
components $\boldsymbol{\tau}$.

A central theoretical property of MLPs is their universal approximation capability
\cite{cybenko1989approximation, hornik1989multilayer}: a feedforward network with
sufficient width and smooth non-linear activations can approximate any continuous
function on a closed domain to an arbitrary degree of accuracy.

\subsubsection{Weight Initialization: Glorot Scheme and Output Zero-Initialization}
\label{subsubsec:weight_initialization}

The initialization of the network weights and biases strongly influences optimization
stability. To avoid vanishing or exploding signals across deep layers, the hidden
layer weights are initialized using the Xavier/Glorot normal distribution
\cite{goodfellow2016deep}, which scales the variance of the initial parameters
according to the width of adjacent layers.

For the final output layers representing the extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$
and the pressure $p$, we adopt a strategic zero-initialization ($\boldsymbol{W}^{(L)}
= \boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{b}^{(L)} = \boldsymbol{0}$). This choice is motivated
by two practical reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Thermodynamic Baseline}: In fluid mechanics, a fluid at thermodynamic
        rest has zero extra-stress ($\boldsymbol{\tau} = \boldsymbol{0}$).
        Zero-initialization guarantees that at iteration zero, the network outputs
        correspond to the unperturbed resting state.
    \item \textbf{Suppression of Numerical Shocks}: If output weights were randomly
        initialized, initial stress predictions would exhibit high-frequency spatial
        noise across the domain. Evaluating these random fluctuations inside
        non-linear constitutive equations—which contain quadratic stress products and
        convective gradients—would cause initial residual losses to spike violently,
        destabilizing the optimization. Initializing the output layer to zero ensures
        smooth, bounded initial residuals.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Loss Functions and Optimization Algorithms}
\label{subsec:optimization_solvers}

Training a neural network consists of finding the optimal set of parameters
$\boldsymbol{\theta} = \{ \boldsymbol{W}^{(l)}, \boldsymbol{b}^{(l)} \}$ that
minimizes a scalar loss function $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$. The loss
measures how well the network's predictions match desired targets, typically using
the Mean Squared Error (MSE):
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) - \boldsymbol{u}_i^{\mathrm{target}} \right\|_2^2 .
    \label{eq:mse_loss}
\end{equation}

The parameters are updated iteratively in the direction that reduces the loss,
following the principle of gradient descent:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L} ,
    \label{eq:gradient_descent_update}
\end{equation}
where $\eta > 0$ is the learning rate, which controls the step size taken along the
negative gradient.

In this work, training is carried out using a two-stage optimization strategy:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{First-Order Optimization (Adam)}: The Adaptive Moment Estimation
        optimizer \cite{kingma2014adam} dynamically adjusts the learning rate for
        each individual parameter based on running estimates of past gradients. This
        makes Adam robust and effective at exploring complex loss landscapes during
        the early stages of training.
    \item \textbf{Second-Order Quasi-Newton Optimization (L-BFGS)}: Once the solution
        is close to a minimum, the Limited-memory Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno
        (L-BFGS) algorithm uses curvature information to achieve faster, super-linear
        convergence. In scientific problems, L-BFGS is crucial for refining the
        solution and driving physical residuals down to high accuracy ($10^{-5} -
        10^{-7}$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Backpropagation and Computational Graphs}
\label{subsec:backpropagation}

To update the parameters via gradient descent, the network must compute the
derivative of the loss with respect to every weight and bias, denoted by
$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}$. Evaluating these derivatives numerically
by perturbing each parameter one by one would be computationally infeasible.

Instead, neural networks compute exact gradients efficiently using the
\textit{backpropagation} algorithm \cite{goodfellow2016deep}. The execution of a
neural network can be viewed as a directed computational graph, where inputs flow
forward through mathematical operations to produce the predictions and the final loss
value. Backpropagation applies the classical chain rule of calculus in reverse order,
starting from the loss and propagating sensitivities backward through the graph.

Through this reverse-mode evaluation, the gradients with respect to all parameters in
the network are computed simultaneously in a single backward pass, at a computational
cost roughly equivalent to that of a single forward evaluation. This computational
efficiency is what makes training deep models practical.

\section{Why Neural Networks? Capabilities and Physical Limitations}
\label{sec:why_neural_networks}

\subsection{Capabilities and Universal Function Approximation}
\label{subsec:capabilities_universal}

Neural networks offer distinctive characteristics that make them attractive for continuous physical modeling:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Continuous and Meshless Representation}: Traditional grid-based methods 
    (Finite Element, Finite Volume, and Finite Difference Methods) represent state variables 
    exclusively at discrete vertices or cell centers, relying on explicit mesh connectivity. 
    In contrast, a neural network provides a continuous, globally defined mapping $\boldsymbol{x} \mapsto \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x})$ 
    over the entire domain, capable of being evaluated at arbitrary spatial coordinates. 
    However, being meshless does not imply freedom from geometric considerations: defining the domain, 
    enforcing boundary conditions, and designing effective collocation point distributions remain essential 
    and influential steps.
    \item \textbf{High-Dimensional Functional Approximation}: The universal approximation capacity 
    allows neural networks to represent complex, coupled physical fields (velocities, pressures, and stress tensors) 
    within a unified functional representation, without requiring specialized interpolation elements for each variable.
    \item \textbf{Hardware Acceleration and Differentiability}: Modern deep learning frameworks 
    are built upon dense linear algebra operations optimized for parallel accelerator hardware (GPUs and TPUs), 
    and provide built-in automatic differentiation for computing spatial derivatives of the represented fields.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Critical Bottlenecks of Purely Data-Driven Machine Learning}
\label{subsec:black_box_limits}

Despite their high expressivity, deploying purely data-driven, black-box neural networks 
to fluid mechanics and non-Newtonian rheology reveals fundamental limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe Data Scarcity in Experimental Rheology}: Deep neural networks 
    conventionally rely on extensive labeled datasets. 
    In experimental fluid mechanics, acquiring dense velocity fields via techniques such as Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) 
    is labor-intensive, while non-intrusive measurements of internal extra-stress components 
    are generally unavailable in complex geometries.
    \item \textbf{Violation of Fundamental Conservation Laws}: Purely data-driven models 
    minimize statistical discrepancy over discrete training points without enforcing physical principles. 
    Consequently, their predictions routinely violate mass conservation ($\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u} \neq 0$), 
    linear momentum balance, and constitutive relationships, leading to unphysical artifacts—particularly 
    when interpolating across sparse regions or extrapolating beyond observed conditions.
    \item \textbf{Overfitting and Spurious Oscillations}: In low-data regimes, over-parameterized networks 
    can readily fit experimental noise or sparse sensor readings with minimal training error, 
    while developing high-frequency, non-physical oscillations in unmonitored subdomains.
    \item \textbf{Inability to Identify Governing Parameters}: Black-box architectures fit mapping functions 
    between inputs and outputs, but cannot infer underlying physical material properties—such as fluid relaxation 
    times or polymeric viscosities—that govern the flow dynamics.
\end{itemize}

These fundamental shortcomings emphasize that data alone are insufficient for scientific modeling in rheology, 
motivating frameworks that embed the governing physical equations directly into the learning process.

\section{The Physics-Informed Neural Network Paradigm}
\label{sec:pinn_paradigm}

The Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) framework, formalized by Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis \cite{raissi2019physics}, 
addresses the limitations of purely data-driven modeling by embedding mathematical governing equations directly into 
the objective function of a neural network.

\subsection{Physics-Constrained Loss Formulation}
\label{subsec:pinn_loss_formulation}

Consider a general non-linear differential operator $\mathcal{F}$ governing a continuous physical state $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})$ 
over a spatial domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ bounded by $\partial \Omega$:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{F}[\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}); \boldsymbol{\lambda}] &= \boldsymbol{0} , \quad \boldsymbol{x} \in \Omega , \label{eq:general_pde_operator} \\
    \mathcal{B}[\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})] &= \boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}) , \quad \boldsymbol{x} \in \partial \Omega , \label{eq:general_bc_operator}
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ represents physical parameters (such as fluid viscosities or relaxation times) 
and $\mathcal{B}$ denotes boundary differential operators.

In a PINN, the unknown solution $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})$ is approximated by a neural network $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta})$. 
Substituting $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ into the governing equations defines the differential residual:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \equiv \mathcal{F}[\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}); \boldsymbol{\lambda}] .
    \label{eq:pde_residual_def}
\end{equation}
The network parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$—and, in inverse problems, the unknown physical parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$—are 
optimized by minimizing a composite multi-objective loss function:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = w_{\mathrm{data}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} + w_{\mathrm{bc}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} + w_{\mathrm{pde}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} ,
    \label{eq:composite_loss_pinn}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{data}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{data}}} \left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_i^{\mathrm{data}}) - \boldsymbol{u}_i^{\mathrm{data}} \right\|_2^2 , \label{eq:loss_data_comp} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{bc}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bc}}} \left\| \mathcal{B}[\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}})] - \boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}}) \right\|_2^2 , \label{eq:loss_bc_comp} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}} &= \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{pde}}} \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{pde}}} \left\| \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}}; \boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \right\|_2^2 . \label{eq:loss_pde_comp}
\end{align}
Here, $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_i^{\mathrm{data}} \}$ denotes available experimental or numerical measurements, 
$\{ \boldsymbol{x}_j^{\mathrm{bc}} \}$ represents points sampled along physical boundaries $\partial \Omega$, 
and $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}} \}$ designates interior \textit{collocation points} scattered across $\Omega$.

This formulation naturally accommodates two distinct problem settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Forward Problems}: The physical parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ are fully known. 
    The network optimizes its weights $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ to solve for the unknown fields $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x})$, 
    acting as a continuous PDE solver.
    \item \textbf{Inverse Problems}: One or more physical parameters in $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ are unknown. 
    The network simultaneously reconstructs the physical fields and identifies the unknown parameters by leveraging 
    both available observations $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}$ and the physical consistency constraints $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}$.
\end{itemize}

A critical distinction must be drawn between reconstructing physical fields and identifying governing parameters. 
A neural network may achieve low training residuals and appear to fit observed velocity fields accurately, 
yet the inferred parameters may remain inaccurate or poorly identified due to parameter correlations, 
insufficient kinematic variation in the data, or ill-conditioned loss landscapes. 
Integrating governing equations provides necessary structure, but does not automatically guarantee 
parameter identifiability or solution uniqueness.

\subsection{Deep Dive: Standard Supervised Training vs. PINN Optimization}
\label{subsec:deep_dive_training}

The operational differences between conventional supervised learning and physics-informed optimization 
are characterized by several key aspects:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Training as a Continuous Boundary-Value Solver vs. Statistical Generalization}: 
    In classical machine learning, a neural network is optimized on a finite training partition with 
    the primary objective of statistical out-of-sample generalization to unseen test distributions ($X_{\mathrm{test}} \to Y_{\mathrm{test}}$). 
    In contrast, in Physics-Informed Neural Networks, \textit{the training process itself is the numerical solver}. 
    The network is not designed as an inductive predictor to infer flow fields across disparate, unseen geometries; 
    rather, the optimization of parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ directly computes the solution of a specific boundary-value problem on the domain $\Omega$. 
    Fitting empirical data $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}$ during training does not function as an external test of generalization, 
    but acts as an active physical regularization and data-assimilation mechanism that guides the optimization manifold 
    toward the true physical state alongside differential residuals $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}$ and boundary conditions $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bc}}$.

    \item \textbf{Physical Regularization and Inductive Bias}: In standard supervised training, 
    the network is unconstrained in regions devoid of training data, which allows over-parameterized models 
    to fit noisy samples while producing spurious oscillations. 
    In PINNs, evaluating the differential residual $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pde}}$ across interior collocation points 
    imposes an analytical inductive bias: functions exhibiting non-physical gradients or curvature 
    incur substantial penalties from the governing differential operators. 
    This physical regularization significantly constrains the admissible solution space. 
    However, it should not be viewed as an absolute guarantee against overfitting: 
    if measurement data are noisy or if loss weights $w_{\mathrm{data}}, w_{\mathrm{pde}}$ are poorly balanced, 
    the optimization can still overfit local measurements or converge to unphysical local minima.

    \item \textbf{Coordinate Automatic Differentiation (AD)}: In conventional deep learning, 
    backpropagation employs AD to differentiate the scalar loss with respect to network parameters:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Backpropagation (Classical DL):} \quad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}} .
    \end{equation}
    In PINNs, the exact same reverse-mode automatic differentiation engine is applied to compute spatial derivatives 
    of the predicted state fields with respect to input coordinates:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Coordinate AD (PINNs):} \quad \frac{\partial \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}}, \quad \frac{\partial^2 \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}^2} .
    \end{equation}
    As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:differentiation_comparison}, coordinate AD evaluates spatial derivatives 
    analytically along the computational graph, avoiding the truncation error $\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2)$ 
    inherent in finite-difference stencils. 
    Nonetheless, coordinate AD remains subject to machine floating-point roundoff, network functional approximation errors, 
    and potential numerical ill-conditioning in regions of steep gradient transitions.

    \item \textbf{Meshless Nature and Point Sampling}: Unlike traditional CFD techniques that rely on 
    conforming discretization meshes and element connectivity tables, PINNs evaluate differential residuals 
    at unorganized collocation point sets $\{ \boldsymbol{x}_k^{\mathrm{pde}} \}$. 
    This meshless nature simplifies point generation in non-trivial domain boundaries. 
    However, it replaces mesh generation with the challenge of point sampling: inadequate collocation density 
    in boundary layers or near stagnation zones can prevent the network from resolving localized physical features.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Foundational Literature Benchmarks}
\label{sec:literature_benchmarks}

\subsection{Navier--Stokes Flow Past a Cylinder (Raissi et al., 2019)}
\label{subsec:cylinder_benchmark}

In their foundational work, Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis \cite{raissi2019physics} 
demonstrated the capability of PINNs on the canonical benchmark of two-dimensional unsteady laminar flow 
past a circular cylinder at Reynolds number $Re = 100$, featuring periodic vortex shedding (the Von Kármán vortex street).

\subsubsection{Problem Formulation and Inverse Discovery}
The non-dimensional incompressible Navier--Stokes equations were formulated as:
\begin{align}
    u_t + \lambda_1 (u u_x + v u_y) &= -p_x + \lambda_2 (u_{xx} + u_{yy}) , \label{eq:cylinder_ns_u} \\
    v_t + \lambda_1 (u v_x + v v_y) &= -p_y + \lambda_2 (v_{xx} + v_{yy}) , \label{eq:cylinder_ns_v} \\
    u_x + v_y &= 0 , \label{eq:cylinder_cont}
\end{align}
where $\lambda_1$ represents the convective acceleration coefficient ($\lambda_1^{\mathrm{true}} = 1.0$) 
and $\lambda_2$ corresponds to the kinematic viscosity ($\lambda_2^{\mathrm{true}} = 1/Re = 0.01$).

To satisfy incompressibility~\eqref{eq:cylinder_cont} identically, Raissi et al. parameterized velocities 
via the scalar stream function formulation $\psi(t, x, y)$ through the exact relations 
$u = \partial\psi/\partial y, v = -\partial\psi/\partial x$ established in Eq.~\eqref{eq:stream_function_def}. 
A single neural network with 9 hidden layers and 20 neurons per layer, activated by hyperbolic tangent ($\tanh$) functions, 
was trained to predict the latent fields $(\psi, p)$ from coordinates $(t, x, y)$ while treating $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ 
as unknown learnable parameters.

\subsubsection{Latent Field Discovery and Parameter Identification}
The network was trained on $N = 5{,}000$ spatio-temporal velocity observations sampled within the vortex street wake. 
This study highlighted two significant capabilities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latent Pressure Field Discovery}: Without any pressure measurements during training, 
    the network reconstructed the spatio-temporal pressure field $p(t, x, y)$ solely through momentum residual enforcement, 
    recovering the reference CFD pressure field up to an arbitrary hydrostatic constant.
    \item \textbf{Parameter Estimation Under Noise}: On clean velocity data, the authors reported relative errors 
    of $0.08\%$ for $\lambda_1$ and $4.67\%$ for $\lambda_2$. When $1\%$ uncorrelated Gaussian noise was introduced, 
    the errors remained controlled at $0.17\%$ for $\lambda_1$ and $5.70\%$ for $\lambda_2$.
\end{itemize}
While these results demonstrated the feasibility of simultaneous field reconstruction and parameter identification, 
the problem was governed by linear viscous stresses in a Newtonian fluid with open boundaries, 
leaving open the question of how such approaches behave under non-Newtonian constitutive equations.

\subsection{The ViscoelasticNet Framework (Thakur et al., 2024)}
\label{subsec:viscoelasticnet_benchmark}

Extending the physics-informed framework to non-Newtonian media, Thakur, Raissi, and Ardekani 
\cite{thakur2024viscoelasticnet} introduced \textit{ViscoelasticNet}, demonstrating the application of PINNs 
to polymeric flows governed by differential constitutive models.

\subsubsection{The Rheological Bottleneck}
Viscoelastic fluid modeling presents severe numerical and experimental challenges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Experimental Limits}: Non-intrusive measurements of the internal extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ 
    are generally unavailable in complex geometries, preventing full empirical characterization of stress fields.
    \item \textbf{Numerical Breakdown (HWNP)}: In conventional grid-based solvers (FEM, FVM), 
    elevated elasticity triggers the High Weissenberg Number Problem (HWNP), where hyperbolic stress advection 
    induces steep gradients that lead to numerical instability and loss of convergence, 
    often requiring artificial stabilization techniques.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{The Virtual Rheometer Concept}
To address these challenges, ViscoelasticNet proposed an inverse framework conceptualized as a "virtual rheometer":
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latent Stress Inference}: Given velocity observations $\boldsymbol{u}$, 
    the framework infers the unmeasured extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ and pressure field $p$ 
    by enforcing constitutive and momentum residuals.
    \item \textbf{Unified Constitutive Formulation}: The authors formulated a unified constitutive law 
    encompassing Oldroyd-B, linear PTT, and Giesekus models:
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{\tau} + \lambda \overset{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{\tau}} + \frac{\alpha \lambda}{\mu_p} (\boldsymbol{\tau} \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau}) + \frac{\varepsilon \lambda}{\mu_p} \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau}) \boldsymbol{\tau} = 2\mu_p \boldsymbol{D} ,
        \label{eq:unified_constitutive_thakur}
    \end{equation}
    seeking to identify the relaxation time $\lambda$, polymeric viscosity $\mu_p$, and non-linear mobility parameters ($\alpha, \varepsilon$).
    \item \textbf{Decoupled Two-Stage Training}: Optimization was structured into two sequential phases:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Stage 1 (Stress and Rheology)}: Velocity and stress networks were optimized jointly 
        using velocity data mismatch and constitutive residuals, identifying rheological parameters $(\lambda, \mu_p, \alpha, \varepsilon)$.
        \item \textit{Stage 2 (Pressure and Viscosity)}: The stress network was frozen, and the pressure network 
        was trained alongside solvent viscosity $\mu_s$ using momentum residuals.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Investigated Geometries and Reported Performance}: In their study, ViscoelasticNet was evaluated 
    on two 2D planar geometries: a channel with sinusoidal stenosis and a cross-slot flow, both under creeping flow conditions. 
    The authors reported relative stress errors below $5\%$ and demonstrated stability under synthetic velocity noise up to $10\%$.
\end{itemize}
These findings established that PINNs can serve as an effective tool for inferring non-Newtonian stresses from velocity fields 
in channel configurations. However, their formulation was tailored to open channels with through-flow and relied on specific 
boundary information, leaving critical methodological questions unaddressed. 

In the subsequent chapter, these open questions are systematically tackled: we define the literature gap, formulate a stream-function PINN framework for the enclosed four-roll mill geometry, and evaluate its performance in forward simulation and inverse constitutive parameter discovery.
