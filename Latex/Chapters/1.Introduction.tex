\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

Non-Newtonian and viscoelastic fluid flows are central to several industrial and biomedical applications, including polymer processing, extrusion, injection molding, and microfluidic lab-on-a-chip devices. Unlike Newtonian fluids, polymer solutions and macromolecular melts exhibit fading memory, non-linear stress-strain relationships, and elasticity. These characteristics give rise to distinctive hydrodynamic phenomena, such as rod climbing (the Weissenberg effect), die swell, and purely elastic flow instabilities that can develop even at vanishing Reynolds numbers where inertia is negligible.

Simulating these flows computationally remains an open challenge in continuum mechanics. Conventional discretization techniques, such as the Finite Element Method (FEM) and the Finite Volume Method (FVM), encounter the High Weissenberg Number Problem (HWNP). In this regime, hyperbolic stress advection generates steep localized gradients and thin boundary layers near stagnation points and solid walls, frequently leading to numerical instability and convergence breakdown. Furthermore, while established computational fluid dynamics packages handle forward simulations where constitutive parameters are known, identifying unknown rheological properties from kinematic observations requires computationally expensive iterative optimization loops.

Over the past decade, Scientific Machine Learning (SciML) and Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) have emerged as an alternative approach for continuum problems. By embedding the governing partial differential equations directly into the network loss through automatic differentiation, PINNs integrate forward flow reconstruction and inverse parameter estimation into a single continuous, mesh-free framework. While earlier benchmarks have focused on open-channel flows with net through-flow, the robustness, training stability, and identifiability limits of PINNs in enclosed recirculating geometries with mixed kinematics remain to be established.

This dissertation investigates the formulation, accuracy, and physical identifiability of physics-informed neural network architectures applied to the four-roll mill geometry, a configuration characterized by a central hyperbolic extensional stagnation point surrounded by shear layers adjacent to rotating cylinders.

\section{Structure of the Dissertation}
\label{sec:dissertation_structure}

The remainder of this dissertation is organized into four sequential chapters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:fluidodynamic_background} (Viscoelastic Fluid Mechanics and the Four-Roll Mill)}: Establishes the continuum mechanics foundation, governing Navier--Stokes equations, and constitutive closures for Oldroyd-B and Giesekus fluids. It formally characterizes the four-roll mill cavity, its kinematic flow classification, and the stream-function parameterization.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:pinn_literature} (Physics-Informed Neural Networks in Complex Rheology)}: Reviews deep feedforward neural architectures, automatic differentiation, and the foundational PINN paradigm. It contrasts traditional machine learning generalization with continuous PDE boundary-value solving, and surveys prior literature benchmarks in computational rheology.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:results_discussion} (Results and Discussion: Four-Roll Mill PINN Solver)}: Formulates the stream-function PINN solver for the four-roll mill and details the staged optimization strategy. It presents rigorous forward validations against high-fidelity COMSOL FEM solutions, inverse parameter estimation across linear and non-linear rheologies, grid independence analyses, transfer learning stability, and the mathematical origin of PTT shear flow degeneracies.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions} (Conclusions and Future Perspectives)}: Synthesizes the core scientific findings of this research, reviews operational limitations regarding boundary stress supervision, and outlines promising future research directions.
\end{itemize}
