\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

Viscoelastic fluids are present across modern industry and everyday consumer
life. Household products, such as toothpaste, mayonnaise, cosmetic gels,
and pharmaceutical creams, alongside industrial polymer solutions, melts, and
biological suspensions, exhibit rheological behaviors fundamentally more complex
than classical Newtonian liquids such as water or light oils \cite{bird1987dynamics,
macosko1994rheology}. While the deviatoric stress in water responds instantaneously
and linearly to the local rate of deformation, viscoelastic materials display
elasticity and a fading memory of their deformation history. Under flow,
internal macromolecular chains and microstructure stretch, align, and store
elastic energy, relaxing over finite timescales toward their equilibrium
conformations. Consequently, the local state of stress depends not only on the
instantaneous velocity gradients, but integrates the entire deformation trajectory
experienced by fluid particles.

Describing the combined effects of viscous dissipation and elastic memory requires
governing equations that involve non-trivial, non-linear mathematics
\cite{owens2002computational}. The hyperbolic transport of stress tensors, the
necessity of material frame-indifferent rates such as the Upper-Convected Time
Derivative (UCTD), and the complex stress saturation closures of non-linear
constitutive models present a fascinating field of study. In
industrial operations, such as polymer extrusion, injection molding, 
film coating, and dispersive micro-mixing, these fluids undergo intense,
coupled shearing and extensional deformations. In these regimes, flow-induced
elastic stresses can trigger macroscopic instabilities, including die swell, melt
fracture, and elastic turbulence, which directly govern product throughput and
material quality.

Characterizing viscoelastic materials is difficult because standard laboratory
rheometers differ kinematically from industrial flow geometries. Conventional
rotational rheometers test fluids almost exclusively in simple shear, where
polymer chains rotate without significant stretching. As a result, shear data
alone cannot capture the extensional response that dominates processing flows
\cite{bird1987dynamics}. A comprehensive study of viscoelastic fluids requires
experimental and numerical geometries that sustain controlled extensional
kinematics away from solid walls while allowing mixed flow regimes.

To address this challenge, we introduce the four-roll mill geometry, originally
presented by Taylor \cite{taylor1934formation} and formalized by
Fuller, Leal, and Bentley \cite{fuller1980flow, fuller1981flow, bentley1986experimental}.
The apparatus consists of a closed square cavity containing four cylindrical rollers
disposed symmetrically around the center. Each roller can rotate independently,
generating a wide variety of planar two-dimensional flow fields inside the cavity,
particularly within the central stagnation region. Denoting the angular velocity
of diagonal rollers 1 and 3 as $\Omega$ and rollers 2 and 4 as $-k\Omega$,
adjusting the kinematic parameter $k$ between $-1$ and $+1$ generates a range of
flow kinematics: pure rotation ($k = -1$), simple shear ($k = 0$) with equal
strain rate and vorticity, and pure planar extension ($k = +1$) where
counter-rotating cylinders create a central hyperbolic stagnation point with pure
elongation along the outflow axis. Intermediate values ($-1 < k < 1$) produce
mixed shear and extensional fields. This kinematic flexibility makes the
four-roll mill a suitable benchmark to study macromolecular deformation and stress
accumulation away from wall boundaries.

Machine learning and neural networks have found growing use in computational
mechanics for data assimilation and surrogate modeling. Standard data-driven
architectures, however, face clear limitations in fluid dynamics. They demand
dense training datasets, can produce unphysical predictions that violate mass
conservation ($\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u} = 0$) or momentum balance, and often
fail to generalize outside the sampled domain.

These limitations motivate the adoption of the Physics-Informed Neural Network
(PINN) framework, formalized by Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis
\cite{raissi2019physics, cuomo2022scientific}. Instead of relying on empirical
data fitting alone, PINNs embed conservation principles and constitutive
differential equations directly into the loss objective through automatic
differentiation \cite{baydin2018automatic}. Minimizing this composite loss anchors
the physical consistency of the predicted fields, reduces the required training
data, and improves extrapolation beyond the measurement points.

The necessity of a physics-informed approach becomes particularly evident when
addressing inverse rheological problems: determining unknown fluid transport
properties and constitutive parameters ($\lambda, \mu_p, \alpha, \varepsilon$)
directly from experimental kinematic observations, such as planar velocity fields
$\boldsymbol{u}(x, y)$ measured via Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV). Standard
Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD), based on mesh discretization schemes such as
the Finite Element Method (FEM) or Finite Volume Method (FVM) \cite{owens2002computational},
is not natively structured for parameter discovery. To identify the physical
parameters that best match experimental velocity data, conventional numerical
solvers require an external, iterative trial-and-error procedure. 
Physics-Informed Neural Networks solve this problem directly: the governing
differential equations and unknown material parameters are optimized
simultaneously during training. Automatic differentiation evaluates the exact
sensitivity of the physical residuals with respect to the parameters
($\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}} \mathcal{L}$), eliminating the need for repeated
forward CFD solves and providing a unified framework for parameter discovery.

Two foundational papers have shaped this research. Raissi et al.\
\cite{raissi2019physics} first demonstrated the efficacy of PINNs by solving
unsteady laminar Newtonian flow past a circular cylinder, demonstrating latent
pressure recovery and parameter estimation from velocity data. Then, Thakur,
Raissi, and Ardekani \cite{thakur2024viscoelasticnet} extended the methodology to
polymeric fluids through the ViscoelasticNet framework, showing that constitutive
equations could be coupled with momentum conservation to infer polymeric stresses
and extract rheological parameters as a virtual rheometer.

However, ViscoelasticNet was evaluated exclusively on open channel geometries,
specifically a stenosed channel and a cross-slot flow. The objective of this
dissertation is to extend the physics-informed framework to a closed,
recirculating four-roll mill cavity with zero net mass throughput. In this
geometry, polymeric stresses are advected along closed streamlines through
alternating extensional and shearing zones without convective transport at open
boundaries. Furthermore, this work expands the parameter space explored in the
literature, testing the framework across higher relaxation times, elevated
viscosities, and strong non-linear mobility regimes up to the theoretical limit
$\alpha = 0.50$, while analyzing the physical limits of inverse identifiability.

To accomplish this objective, this thesis develops, validates, and analyzes a
physics-informed neural network framework engineered for continuous latent field
reconstruction and inverse rheological characterization in the four-roll mill.
The architecture comprises three specialized, decoupled Multi-Layer Perceptrons
representing the stream function $\psi$, hydrodynamic pressure $p$, and
polymeric extra-stress tensor $\boldsymbol{\tau}$. Parameterizing the velocity
field through $\psi(x, y)$ guarantees exact incompressibility
($\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u} \equiv 0$) by construction, while hydrostatic
pressure indeterminacy is resolved using an algebraic single-point anchor directly
within the forward pass. Optimization follows a staged schedule, combining initial
exploration and second-order quasi-Newton refinement, supported by transfer
learning across elasticity regimes.

We validate the solver against finite element solutions generated in COMSOL
Multiphysics \cite{comsol_multiphysics} in two operating modes. In direct mode,
the network reconstructs latent stress and pressure fields from planar velocity
observations for known rheological parameters. In inverse mode, serving as a
virtual rheometer, the model estimates unknown constitutive parameters while
reconstructing internal stress fields, tested across different fluid models,
collocation densities, and with transfer learning protocol.
